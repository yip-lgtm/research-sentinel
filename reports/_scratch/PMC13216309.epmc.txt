Personal armors were initially designed to fit against the male torso, as men previously filled roles requiring them, thus not made to accommodate the female anatomy. As a result, armor worn by female warfighters tends to have non-form fit in the waist, armpit, bust, and collarbone areas, causing reduced coverage. Shifting, riding up, air gaps, breathing difficulty, and physical discomfort have also been observed. Although this has been documented, the impact on the armor’s protective capabilities has not been well studied. To address this, this study compared the protective capabilities of unisex armor for male and female warfighters from shock threats. Instrumented manikins were exposed to free-field blasts to assess pressure exposure in as-worn conditions. Directionality effects were considered in this study, using five directions from the shock source. It was found that the most harmful exposure to both warfighters occurred during a head-on interaction, with average impulse values 9.7%-72.6% higher than other orientations and peak pressures being 111.0 and 107.9 kPa, respectively. Moreover, a female body shape altered armor fit to increase impulses by up to 78.6%, relative to no armor being worn. This result was not observed for the male warfighter, though peak pressures were reduced for both. As a result, many female warfighters wear unisex armor designed based on the male torso and scaled to smaller sizes 3 – 6 . Observed correlations for these armors include them (1) mainly being reported as oversized 2 and (2) having fit issues dependent on breast size 3 . Specifically, female warfighters with larger breasts tend to report armor being too small, with the opposite trend observed for women with smaller breast sizes 3 . This non-form fit is due to the anthropometric differences between male and female warfighters (i.e., waist circumference, chest depth, trunk mass, and overall proportions 1 – 3 . Specific areas of non-form fit have been observed in the waist, armpit, and bust areas 2 , 4 , 5 , where movement leads to the amor shifting, riding up, and having reduced coverage from threats 1 . Lowered performance, physical discomfort, and breathing difficulty due to pressure on the bust region, as well as air gaps between the wearer and armor, have also been observed 1 , 4 , 5 . These unisex armors may compromise the safety and function of female warfighters in battlefield situations 1 , 3 . Air gaps in non-form-fitting armor pose a significant problem to warfighters due to the reduced coverage and protection against ballistic threats 1 , 7 . However, they may also pose an issue when defending against shock threats if a shock wave enters an air gap and becomes trapped, causing a reverberating pressure wave 8 , 9 . This reverberation increases the pressure and duration of the original wave during each shock reflection 10 , 11 . While not comprehensively studied, it is speculated that high-pressure regions may form between the torso and armor with air gaps present, potentially resulting in behind-armor injuries in a similar manner as the underwash effect inside the cavity of a warfighters helmet 12 . So far, reflections between flush material interfaces have been speculated to increase injury risk by introducing multiple peak pressures 13 . However, air gaps were not considered, and pressure data was not collected. Despite its potential threat, little research has been conducted on the effects of air gaps in non-form-fitting armor or sex-based differences in armor protection and exposure. Quantifying the effect of non-form-fitting armor and air gaps can be done using shock tubes or free-field blasts with piezoelectric sensors 14 . Methodologies incorporating these aspects have been implemented in armor 14 and bomb suit testing 15 and have been used to quantify the underwash effect in a helmet cavity 12 . From such studies, a reduced injury risk for warfighters has been identified as lower peak pressure and impulse, as well as increased rise time and duration 15 . Directionality from a shock source has been shown to have significant effects on the resulting exposure. While body armor is relatively untested, orientation effects on the exposure underneath the helmet has been studied, where 0- and 180-degrees resulted in the highest peak pressures and impulses 8 , 12 , 16 . The oblique reflections off the helmet/head had also been shown to be affected from the change in incidence angle 8 , 12 . These reflections are caused by the incident shock wave interacting with a surface at an angle, having the flow deflect unevenly across the shock wave to cause variations in pressure exposure and reflection behavior 17 . This study used free field blasts to expose instrumented manikins to shock wave pressures to examine sex-based differences in energy distribution experienced by male and female warfighters that may lead to primary blast injury. However, in this study, a single male and female shape was used to assess potential sex-based effects, thus not providing a comprehensive representation of all warfighter shapes. Directionality from the shock source was assessed by orienting manikins at 0, 45, 90, 135, and 180-degrees from a Composition C4 (C4) charge. Both manikins were equipped with small arms protective insert (SAPI) armor and evaluated with and without it worn. Comparisons between the armors’ protective capabilities were performed using the male manikin as a baseline due to the armor having a male-based (unisex) design. Methods In this study, an experimental approach was employed to gather quantitative data on the shock exposure differences between male and female warfighters equipped with commercially available unisex SAPI armor. Thirty free-field blasts were performed to expose instrumented male and female manikins to pressures at varied orientations relative to the blast. Both manikins were tested simultaneously, and three repeats were conducted for each orientation. Tests were repeated with and without body armor worn as animal-based studies suggest that body armor only protects against the initial shock load but does not protect against primary blast injuries due to reverberating pressure waves 13 . Test manikins Test manikins were obtained from Safeguard Medical Simbodies, having been selected as they were designed to be geometrically representative of the male and female body shapes. The female manikin was an early-version full-body advanced model 18 and a male torso was an early-version half-body task trainer 19 , with no head and the legs ending 6.5 cm past the crotch area. Partial arms were included on the male manikin, extending nearly to the elbows, ending at the bottom of the armor plate worn during testing. The manikins incorporated a silicone skin model that closely mimicked the look and feel of flesh, 3D-printed rib cages, and silicone breast structures.  To enable data collection, the manikins were instrumented with PCB flush mount sensors (Model 102B18 21 with locations based on anthropometric landmarks and areas of expected differences in pressure distribution, as shown in Fig. 1 . Many locations were selected based on reported areas of non-form fit for female warfighters wearing body armor, with the chest and collarbone areas of particular interest. Sensors were placed at the upper and lower sternum (Sensors 1 and 6), nipples (Sensors 2 and 3), and underbust region (Sensors 4 and 5). The sensor on the lower sternum was placed in each torso’s geometric center for data collection and as a reference point when measuring distances from the charges. Between the manikins, there was approximately a half-inch vertical difference in sensor placement, using the chest circumference line as a reference point.

 During testing, the manikins were mounted at the average shoulder height for adults in the United States, approximately 144 cm for men and 133 cm for women 20 . They were both equipped with Highcom 4S16 multi-curve SAPI armor from Apex Armor Solutions 22 . The male wore a medium-sized plate, and the female wore a small-sized plate, with sizes determined based on the circumference of the chest at its fullest point 23 . The difference in coverage between the two plate sizes was a 1.9 cm difference in height and width. Visible air gaps were observed between the manikin torsos and body armor, especially throughout the lower torso for the female manikin. The thickness of these air gaps for both manikins was quantified by separately wrapping the torsos and armor in heat-shrinking plastic for a tight fit. The armor and torsos were then layered with a gap-filling spray foam and allowed to cure in their as-worn condition. Once cured for 24 h, the foam pieces were removed to measure the foam thickness at each sensor position and other points of interest.  Energetic charge The manikins were exposed to repeatable estimated pressures of 92 kPa static (side-on) (250 kPa reflected (head-on)), using 260 g C4 charges hung from a steel stand 2 m away from the geometric center of each manikin and at a height of 0.3 m, regardless of orientation. Charge parameters were selected to ensure that the Mach stem, the intersection of the incident shock wave and the ground reflection, occurred above the manikins. This allowed for a single, flatter shock wave interaction with the manikin. Triple point height calculations followed UFC-340-02 24 , which relied on Swisdak data 25 . Experimental setup The experimental setup consisted of two manikins placed 2 m from the charge, with their geometric centers used as a reference point. A control stand was included with a 137B23B pencil probe 26 to record side-on pressures for repeatability and verify manikin exposure. From the thirty tests, the pencil probe recorded a 100.2 ± 4.2 kPa static pressure, with no outliers detected. Pressure sensor cables were connected to a Synergy P Hi Techniques Data Acquisition System 27 . A monochrome Phantom V2012 high-speed camera was used at 62,000 fps to capture the shock interactions and reflections 28 , which were visualized with background-oriented schlieren (BOS) using a random dot-patterned banner, designed for a 3–5 pixel dot diameter in the videos 29 . Testing was conducted over three days with temperatures ranging between 22 and 25 °C. Statistical analysis of the control data revealed statistical similarity across the three days. Throughout testing, the manikins and body armor were not replaced after each test due to all measurement points being external, making potential change in the material properties not a concern. Regarding the body armor, post-testing inspection showed no evidence of cracking, chipping, or delamination of the plates, and the exposures throughout testing were far below the impact pressures the plates are designed to defeat during ballistic impact 22 . The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2 .

 Manikin orientations relative to the blast were varied throughout testing to assess the armor’s protective capabilities. Orientations included were 0 (head-on), 45, 90, 135, and 180-degrees, as areas of probable non-form fit would be more exposed to direct energy transfer from the blasts at oblique orientations. A 180-degree orientation was included as literature observations note that armors are often too wide and long for female warfighters 3 , 4 . It was expected that this overhung armor would allow shock wave energy to reflect off of it when the manikins were oriented at 180 degrees, redirecting the energy to enter the space between the torso and armor, forming regions of high pressure. Tests were performed with and without armor, with the male used as a baseline in both cases. Three repeats were carried out for each variation, totaling 30 tests. Results and discussion The analysis of time-pressure data was performed by assessing the effect of wearing armor on the time-pressure waveforms, detailing the exposure for both a male and female manikin. Pressure distribution comparisons were then made based on sex, directionality, and pressure sensor location. Armor effects on time-pressure waveforms Trials were performed by orienting the manikins with and without body armor at orientations 0, 45, 90, 135, and 180-degrees relative to the shock source. Differences in the waveforms were observed using an average of three trials. Results using the upper sternum (Sensor 1) for each orientation is shown in Fig.  3 .  Without body armor, time-pressure waveforms for male and female warfighters generally had higher peak pressures and faster rise times. An example is shown in Fig. 3 B, where an 89.7% and 52.4% drop in peak pressure occurred at a 45-degree orientation with armor worn by the male and female manikins, respectively. These pressure drops were expected, as animal-based shock studies suggest that armor dissipates energy from the initial load but can prolong exposure times by trapping energy 13 . Compared to known injury correlations for primary blast injuries, these reductions suggest a greatly reduced risk of lung injury for warfighters wearing armor by reducing the initial blast load past the current peak pressure threshold for lung-hemorrhaging of 207 to 276 kPa (30–40 psi) for short-duration exposures of 3–5 ms 24 . However, these reduced peak pressures were paired with rise times that increased by 0.29 and 0.44 ms for the male and female warfighters, compared to their near-instantaneous counterparts (0.018 ms and 0.013 ms) when armor was not worn. This increased rise time was expected as the armor would trap energy between the torso and armor, causing a more gradual buildup in pressure and potentially multiple peak pressures (Fig. 3 ) that could still damage the heart and lungs 13 . Considering impulses without armor, the male warfighter generally experienced higher impulses, despite the manikins being placed at the same distance from the shock source. At a head-on orientation, the male warfighter experienced an average impulse of 65.7 kPa*ms (72.1 kPa*ms maximum at Sensor 4) while the female received an average impulse of 59.5 kPa*ms (62.4 kPa*ms maximum at Sensor 4). At side-on orientation, however, the experienced impulses were very similar, being 38.0 and 37.2 kPa*ms for the male and female warfighter, respectively. A potential factor contributing to these exposure differences was their physical size, as Gordon’s anthropometric survey comparing male and female warfighters found that male warfighters had larger body dimensions in 94% of all torso-specific cases 20 . These dimensional differences lead to male warfighters often having larger torso surface areas, which may be a potential factor in male-female warfighter exposure differences. This is speculated to be the case because, at a 0-degree orientation, the male and female warfighters had a 25.5% difference in torso surface area facing the charge (0.19 and 0.14 m 2 , respectively). In contrast, at a 90-degree orientation, their surface areas were 0.067 m 2 (male) and 0.066 m 2 (female). In both cases, the male warfighter had the largest torso surface area and experienced the highest impulses, suggesting that surface area may be a possible mechanism for the exposure differences between male and female warfighters. With armor worn, armor was shown to increase the impulse (Fig.  3 ) in nearly 73% of instances for the female warfighter, with exceptions mainly pertaining to Sensors 2 and 4, as they were further from the shock source at oblique orientations. On average, impulses increased by 7.5% (0-degrees; Fig.  3 A) to 78.6% (135-degrees; Fig.  3 D) for each orientation, with the highest increase observed at 135-degrees due to the large air gap at Sensor 5 of 2.17 cm. Individual sensor increases ranged from − 13.7% to 379.9%, with an average of 31.3%. Comparatively, the male experienced reductions in impulse in 56.7% of instances, with the majority resulting from more head-on orientations. These reductions individually ranged between 1.4% (Sensor 2; 45-degrees) to 75.9% (Sensor 5; 45-degrees) as shown in Fig.  3 B. However, at orientations exceeding 90-degrees, the impulses experienced by Sensors 3 and 5 greatly increased by up to 400% at 135-degrees due to their proximity and exposure to the shock source, resulting in an average increase of 103% in this orientation. These differences are likely attributed to changes in armor fit, where large air gaps (Table  2 ) present between the armor and female torso may have better trapped energy, sustaining pressure longer in most orientations, demonstrated by the increased durations in Fig.  3 B-E. In contrast, smaller air gaps were present for the male warfighter, which allowed for substantial increases in Sensors 3 and 5 as they were closer to the shock source and exposed in oblique orientations. Orientation-based effects observed for the male and female manikins primarily consisted of reduced peak pressures and impulses as orientation deviated from a head-on shock interaction (Fig. 3 B-E). This is illustrated in Fig. 3 , where it is observed that a 0-degree orientation results in average peak pressures between 9.7% and 72.6% higher than those of other orientations (45 and 180-degrees). This is likely due to reflected pressures generally being of higher magnitude than side-on 24 or oblique exposures 17 , and the energy transmitted through the armor outweighing the energy transfer permitted by the air gaps and diffraction around the armor edges when these areas are more exposed. At 180-degrees, average peak pressures across the torso for the male and female warfighters were the lowest, being 67.8 and 62.5 kPa, respectively. This is due to the shock having to diffract around the torso of either warfighter to interact with the sensors, as no direct path existed. The increased need for diffraction to occur for the shock to interact with the sensors results in lower exposure measurements in orientations deviating from 0-degrees, which makes the 0-degree orientation (Fig. 3 A) likely more harmful to warfighters, as it has higher peak pressures and impulses experienced on the front of the torso. This is important to note that previous studies investigating primary blast injuries have speculated that head-on orientations with shock energy have greater risks to warfighters as it provides the most efficient energy transfer to the heart and lungs, thus resulting in more severe injuries and higher mortality rates 30 . Sex-based differences in pressure distribution Sex-based differences were observed in the overall shock exposure received when wearing armor. The male, with a more ideal fit, resulted in higher average pressure exposures at nearly all orientations than the female manikin. The only exception was 135-degrees where average peak pressures were 71.4 kPa and 68.3 kPa for the female and male warfighters, respectively. However, the extended durations observed for the female warfighter resulted in impulses that were higher than those for the male. These differences in energy distribution for the manikins are illustrated in Figs.  4 and 5 as heatmaps, using the average values (Supplementary Information) of the three trials performed for peak pressure and impulse.

 From the heatmaps in Figs. 4 and 5, the distributions of exposure differed between male and female warfighters in the underbust area (Sensors 4–6). Here, the female consistently had higher impulses than the male warfighter, ranging up to a 35.7% difference (Sensor 6; Figs. 4 C and 5 C) at a 90-degree orientation. This is likely due to the female breasts pushing out the plate in the underbust area, resulting in more continuous air gaps that were on average 98% thicker than those of the male warfighter in this area. In previous works, air gaps were noted to decrease pressure but increase the duration of the exposure, potentially increasing impulse 31 . Likely, the male warfighter, who had a closer form-fit with the armor, would have higher peak pressures and shorter durations with this closer fit than the female, who showed higher impulses and longer durations in these areas. This was also observed on the upper sternum (Sensor 1), as the male warfighter frequently experienced higher pressures, whereas the female warfighter had higher impulses due to the plate being pushed outward, prolonging exposure by slowing the decay back to ambient conditions. At the bust region (Figs. 4 and 5 ; Sensors 2 and 3), the male warfighter experienced higher pressures and impulses on the side closer (Sensor 3) to the shock source. Specifically, pressures from Sensors 3 and 5 for the male warfighter were 24.1% to 40.2% and 9.9% to 56.2% higher, respectively, than the female warfighter, at oblique orientations exceeding 90 degrees. Impulses in this orientation range were 11.7% to 15.7% and − 0.4% to 13.3% higher for the male warfighter at these sensors. This may be due to a known flush fit in the bust area for the female warfighter, as it extends outward more than the rest of the torso. In contrast, this was not guaranteed for the male warfighter, whose chest is more planar. A flush fit on the female warfighter would likely reduce the impulse transmitted through the armor in that area and limit diffraction around that armor edge to the sensor, creating a seal. However, if the male sensor lacked a flush fit in that area, a higher transmitted impulse through that air gap may occur along with diffraction around the armor edge to the sensor. This is possible as armor plates cover vital areas, extending at least from nipple to nipple for optimal coverage 23 . This places the nipples near the edges of the armor plates, so a shock wave diffracting around this area would have little distance to travel before reaching that sensor. Overall energy distribution Effects from orientation changes were further summarized based on holistic depictions of the energy exposure to identify potentially more harmful exposures when wearing armor (Fig.  6 ). This analysis was performed by applying calculating average values across the entire torso and considering distance from the shock source as the manikins were rotated around the lower sternum (Sensor 6). Calculations for the total torso considered Sensors 1–6, the torso side further from the shock source used Sensors 2 and 4, and the side closer used Sensors 3 and 5, and the center used Sensors 1 and 6. There was an approximate 21 cm difference in distance between the far and near sides of the torso at 90 degrees.

 As expected, the energy distribution of both warfighters depended on their orientation relative to the shock source, with more balanced distributions at 0 and 180-degree orientations. This is shown at 0-degrees as the total pressure exposure on the female warfighter was 107.9 kPa, with a breakdown of 110.5 kPa and 97.5 kPa for the near and far sides, respectively. A similar distribution was observed for the male warfighter with a total pressure exposure of 107.9 kPa. This was expected as both sides of the torso were at the same proximity and had the same exposure through air gaps. However, the total impulse at 180-degrees was significantly higher for the male (48.7 kPa*ms for the male vs. 41.1 kPa*ms for the female). This was likely due to the seal the female bust region provided with the armor, which prevented energy infiltration to an extent. In contrast, this effect was absent for the male warfighter as there was an air gap 0.51 cm thick at that area (Sensor 3), allowing for direct energy transfer to the exposed side of the torso. Regression analysis revealed that the total pressure distribution for both warfighters followed a general linear trend, decreasing in magnitude as orientation changed (Fig.  6 A). While this was expected, the individual segments (far, near, and center) of the torso individually showed pressure reductions that followed second-degree polynomial trends (Fig.  6 A). This difference was most evident at 90-degrees, where the pressure in most cases reversed their trends to then either increase or decrease in magnitude, which was expected as at 90-degrees the far and near sides experienced their maximum distance difference. Calculations at the sternum (center), however, reversed trends at 135-degrees, resulting in an increase in pressure for both warfighters at 180-degrees. This increase was likely driven by several factors: the equal proximity of the far and near sides to the shock source; a shift in sensor exposure from a mix of side-on and head-on pressures to predominantly head-on; and diffraction effects that enabled the shock waves to wrap around the torso and infiltrate the armor. These individual segments, when combined, result in a linear trend for the overall distribution, indicating that while orientation-based effects on the total exposure across the torso may lead to a linear reduction of pressure, individual sections exhibit polynomial-type trends that may make oblique orientations more hazardous to warfighters. An example of this is shown in Fig.  6 A, comparing the male warfighter’s total (87.9 kPa) and near-side (163.8 kPa) exposure at 90-degrees, as the near side reaches its peak value here. Regarding impulse, the overall distribution tended to decrease with orientation changes (Fig.  6 B), although distinct and consistent trends were not frequently observed. This was likely due to changes in exposure duration, particularly when pressure became trapped between the torso and armor at oblique angles. These changes resulted in unexpected impulse patterns that complicated the assessment of orientation effects. This was shown as the center and total male warfighter’s exposure increased at 180-degrees, whereas the female warfighter did not. Calculations related to these trends reveal that the lower sternum (Sensor 6) individually followed a clear increasing polynomial-type trend for the male warfighter, though the female warfighter reduced at 180-degrees. Due to the lack of distinct trends, repeated-measures ANOVA tests were conducted to assess orientation-based effects. These tests indicated strong orientation-based effects in the in impulse distributions across all the orientations for both the male (F(4, 20) = 17.23, p-value = < 0.001, generalized eta squared (ges) = 0.69) and female (F(4, 20) = 32.39, p-value = < 0.001, ges = 0.85) warfighters.  Following the initial shock interaction, separate reflections from the armor originated at both the top and bottom edges of the plate (Fig. 7 ). At the top, a reflection propagated at an upward angle, interacting with the chin and head of the female warfighter, and likely would have been observed for the male warfighter. While not the primary focus of this study, such reflections may result in additional shock transmission to the head, potentially increasing the risk of blast-induced traumatic brain injuries. At the bottom of the plate, evidence of shock wave diffraction was observed, with the wave curling up from beneath the plate (Fig. 7 C and G). This behavior is plausible, as the reflected shock exited the armor and encountered an inflection point—the lower edge of the plate—which enabled the wave to change direction (Fig. 7 D and H). Upon reaching this edge, the shock wave diffracted, redirecting upwards by adhering to and riding up the armor’s front side. This phenomenon is consistent with fundamental fluid dynamic principles, where diffracted waves follow surfaces due to pressure gradients 32 . Ultimately, this upward-propagated diffraction merged with the reflection originating from the top of the plate and the one from the lower body. This was observed for both manikins despite the male manikin only having partial legs (6.5 cm past the crotch) due to the plate ending around the naval, leaving much of the lower torso and pelvic region exposed to create a similar shock reflection. Based on high-speed video measurements and the ground as a reference line, there was a suggested 3.1° difference (12.1° female; 9° male) in static armor fit between the male and female warfighters. This was expected, as the recommended worn position of the armor plate has the top start at one’s collarbone 23 , which limits the shoulder strap length on the plate carriers and creates a harsher plate angle for the female warfighter due to their breasts. To accommodate this, the plate would be pushed away from the lower torso, resulting in a closer fit in the collarbone area than in the underbust for female warfighters, however, large air gaps were still observed throughout the collarbone. This agrees with the air gap measurements in Table 2 , as air gaps up to 2.97 cm thick occurred in the lower torso for the female warfighter compared to 1.59 cm for the male (60.5% difference). In the collarbone area, there was a 13.7% difference between the two points, a 2.41 cm thick air gap measured for the female manikin (2.10 cm for the male). Dynamically, however, the harsher plate angle present in static conditions for the female warfighter displayed greater changes when exposed to shock energy. This was demonstrated by movement post-shock interaction (measured over 360 frames after the initial interaction from a single video), where the bottom of the plate moved 8.5 mm towards the torso of the female manikin. This compares to the 7.5 mm movement towards the torso for the male manikin, 11.7% lower than that of the female. This difference is likely due to the silicone breasts of the female manikin that allowed deformation under loading. Such observations would likely translate to female warfighters in battlefield situations, as breasts consist of soft, pliable tissues that deform under gravitational and compressive loads 33 . Conclusions Thirty free-field blasts were conducted to compare differences in pressure transmission from shock threats between a single male and female warfighter shape wearing commercially available SAPI body armor. It was found that peak pressures were reduced for both warfighters wearing body armor, with head-on shock interactions (0-degree orientation) resulting in the worst overall exposures (111.0 and 107.9 kPa for the male and female warfighters). This suggests that the direct transmission of the energy through the armor outweighs infiltration at side-on orientations through the air gaps and diffraction around the armor edges. Sex-based differences resulting from the female body shape were observed, with total impulses increasing by 7.5% to 78.6% for each orientation with armor worn. This compares with the individual sensor increases as they ranged from − 13.7% to 379.9% when armor was worn, demonstrating that while there were overall increases in impulse, the magnitude was dependent on the location of the torso. These occurrences were absent from the male both individually and overall. From the high-speed videos, it was shown that the breasts of the female warfighter pushed the armor away nearly 1.5 cm further from the torso than the male, resulting in a 3.1° greater deviation from the intended angle of the plate. This resulted in larger air gaps being formed between the female torso and armor and allowed for longer entrapment of the energy during exposure, increasing the impulse experienced relative to the male. Further research in this area is required to understand the effect of breast size on female warfighters, as this study was limited to a single male and female shape. This will allow for more informed conclusions regarding the safety of female warfighters equipped with unisex protective gear.
