Summary Craniocerebral and pulmonary injuries are primary blast-induced damages, assessed via numerical simulations, animal models, and postmortem human surrogates (PMHS). Recent years, the successful development of shock wave cell models and organoid models has provided new research directions for evaluation of blast injuries. Particularly human-derived organoids that can highly simulating the structure and function of human organs, significantly enhancing the physiological relevance of the models. Additionally, AI-based models (machine/deep learning) show promise in blast injury prediction and assessment. This review systematically summarizes the biological effects of explosive shock waves, the application of conventional assessment models and their limitations, and emerging technologies—cell/organoid models and AI applications. The utilization of cell models, human-derived organoid models, and AI models for the assessment of blast-induced biological injuries and subsequent research holds significant importance for understanding the cellular mechanisms of injury, protective research, and injury warning systems. Introduction Blast wave injury, also referred to as primary blast injury or blast trauma, is caused by the direct impact of high-pressure gases and shock waves generated instantaneously during an explosion. Such injuries frequently occur in military conflicts, terrorist attacks, industrial accidents, and civilian explosions. Statistical data from recent regional military conflicts indicate that the incidence of blast wave-induced injuries has reached 50%. 1 Based on the injury site, blast injuries primarily include blast-induced traumatic brain injury (bTBI) and lung injury (BLI), among others. 2 According to the experience of a level I trauma center in southern Thailand in treating bTBI, primary blast injury is the most common (48.6%) cause of injury, followed by secondary blast injury (37.1%). Post-injury physical symptoms in patients mainly include scalp injuries, loss of consciousness, and headaches, with the most common pathological manifestations being cerebral contusions (63.0%), subarachnoid hemorrhage (54.3%), and subdural hematoma (47.8%). 3 Patients with BLI often show no obvious external chest trauma, but physical examinations may reveal symptoms, such as tachypnea, hypoxia, cyanosis, and diminished breath sounds. Within 48 h post-injury, symptoms such as chest pain, coughing, and hemoptysis may appear, with severe cases potentially developing air embolism, which can affect vital organs like the heart and brain, posing life-threatening risks. 4 Therefore, establishing appropriate blast injury assessment models and accurately evaluating and studying these injuries hold significant guiding importance in both military and civilian contexts. Currently, research on blast injuries primarily focuses on the injury mechanisms, protective strategies, and treatment methods for craniocerebral and pulmonary blast injuries. However, some perspectives suggest that existing blast injury research models fail to adequately simulate human data, resulting in a low clinical translation rate of related research findings. Based on this, this review takes craniocerebral and pulmonary blast injuries as entry points, systematically introduces the injury effects of these two types of blast injuries, and comprehensively summarizes recent advancements in the development of blast injury assessment models. In addition to animal models, human cadaver models, and numerical simulation models, this review emphasizes the application and research outcomes of human-derived cell models, organoid models, and artificial intelligence models. Furthermore, this paper provides an in-depth analysis of the advantages and limitations of various model systems and systematically categorizes their applicable scenarios. Finally, this review prospectively discusses the technical challenges and future development prospects of cell models, organoid models, and machine learning models in the field of blast injury assessment, aiming to provide theoretical foundations and technical references for future research in this domain. Biological damage effects of blast shock waves bTBI and BLI are characterized by inconspicuous external damage, severe internal injuries, high mortality rates, and challenges in early diagnosis. Some patients may also exhibit delayed onset of symptoms. 5 , 6 Regarding the injury effects of blast waves on the brain and lungs, researchers worldwide have conducted extensive studies, achieving significant progress in understanding the patterns, mechanisms, and biological characteristics of blast wave injuries. 6 , 7 , 8 BTBI BTBI is a common type of injury in modern warfare. The primary mechanism involves the transmission of blast-generated shock waves through various pathways into the intracranial space, forming stress waves that interact with brain tissue. 9 These injuries are often concealed, delayed, and accompanied by multiple complications and long-term sequelae. According to a report by the U.S. Department of Defense, 82.3% of soldiers involved in Operation Iraqi Freedom (OIF), Operation Enduring Freedom (OEF), and Operation New Dawn (OND) were diagnosed with bTBI, 10 and approximately 8.4% of OIF/OEF/OND veterans were diagnosed with blast-induced brain injury. 11 , 12 Mild brain injuries typically manifest as transient confusion, anxiety, headaches, and irritability, with symptoms improving within hours or days. In contrast, severe blast-induced brain injuries, in addition to evident pathological features, such as cerebral edema and intracranial hemorrhage, are often accompanied by headaches, coma, memory loss, and other symptoms of varying duration and intensity. 13 , 14 Studies indicate that approximately 15% of patients with mild blast-induced brain injuries experience long-term symptoms, such as headaches, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, memory impairment, or other cognitive dysfunctions. 15 , 16 Given the high incidence of blast-induced brain injuries and their profound impact on long-term health, systematic research in this area is crucial. BLI Blast waves primarily target gas-containing organs, with the lungs being one of the most affected. Due to the concealed nature, rapid progression, and high mortality rate of blast-induced lung injuries, they have garnered significant attention in both military and medical fields. In the 1996 Jerusalem civilian bus bombing, 15 out of 18 survivors (83.3%) were diagnosed with varying degrees of lung injury. 17 Scott et al. 18 analyzed casualties from an explosion during the Afghanistan conflict and found that 31 out of 238 non-survivors (13%) had primary blast lung injuries identified during autopsies. Among survivors, the proportions of patients with mixed-type lung injuries and pure blast lung injuries were 6.7% and 2.9%, respectively. McDonald Johnston and Ballard 19 conducted chest X-rays and computed tomography (CT) scans on a soldier injured by a vehicle-borne improvised explosive device (IED), revealing patchy diffuse opacities in both lungs, indicative of bilateral pulmonary hemorrhage. Therefore, comprehensive assessment and in-depth research on blast-induced lung injuries are urgently needed. Given the complex nature and high mortality rates associated with blast-induced brain and lung injuries, there is an urgent need to develop diversified research models to achieve precise evaluation and systematic investigation of blast wave-induced biological damage. Assessment models Blast injury assessment models are systematic research tools designed to quantify the extent of damage caused by explosive shock waves on biological organisms or tissues. As early as 1970, Pince 20 classified blast injury research models into four types: (1) human models (e.g., trauma victims); (2) anthropomorphic models (AAMs); (3) live animals; and (4) non-physical models (mathematical or computational models). Anthropomorphic models typically refer to dummy models that are anatomically highly similar to humans, and thus cadaver models can also be included in this category. 21 Due to ethical constraints and the unique nature of explosive events, human models are scarce and unsuitable for blast injury research. With advancements in in vitro culture technologies, cell and organoid models have gradually been applied to blast injury studies. In summary, this section will provide a detailed description of the applications and research outcomes of numerical simulation models, animal models, human cadaver models, cell models, organoid models, and artificial intelligence models in the study of craniocerebral and pulmonary blast injuries. Additionally, the advantages, limitations, and translational potential of each model for blast injury research will be analyzed. Numerical simulation models Numerical simulation models, also known as finite element models, discretize complex structures into a finite number of elements and use mathematical equations to solve their mechanical responses under specific loads. 22 Numerous head finite element models have been developed to study the injury mechanisms and protective strategies for blast-induced brain injuries. Researchers using a rat head finite element model discovered that the primary pathway for blast overpressure transmission to the brain is through the skull, with the nose assisting in diffracting incoming shock waves toward the skull. 23 Further 3D finite element modeling of the human head successfully simulated the deformation of the skull, the evolution of pressure, and the strain distribution in different brain tissues under shock wave loading. 24 , 25 Chafi et al. 26 used a human head finite element model to investigate the feasibility of intracranial pressure (ICP), maximum shear stress, and principal strain as primary predictors for assessing brain injury during the early stages of blast exposure. Numerical simulation models have also been instrumental in evaluating the protective performance of helmets and face shields against blast loads. Sarvghad-Moghaddam et al. 27 found that helmets can block most of the blast shock waves at the front and top, while face shields are only effective in frontal blasts. Interestingly, although head protection equipment can shield brain tissue from injury by blocking shock waves, the resulting downwash overpressure can increase internal helmet pressure, leading to skull deformation and brain tissue damage. 28 Therefore, improvements in internal structure and materials are necessary in helmet design. Finite element models of other organs, such as the lung, have also been developed. Yang et al. 29 constructed a finite element model of blast-induced lung injury in rats, simulating the pressure distribution on the body surface and lungs at different distances (40 cm, 50 cm, and 60 cm). The results showed a high correlation between simulated pressures and pathological scores; the model also predicted higher pressures in the medial and basal regions of the lungs, which are more prone to injury, a finding confirmed by anatomical observations. The primary pathological manifestations included alveolar wall and capillary rupture, hemorrhage, and edema. By constructing a finite element model of blast-induced lung injury using sheep CT data and validating it experimentally, it was found that blast-induced lung injuries in sheep were also concentrated in the medial and basal regions, and the model could predict the asymmetry of left and right lung injuries. 30 Numerical simulation models, based on computer graphics and simulation technologies, can dynamically simulate the pressure distribution and stress changes during the transmission of blast shock waves from the body surface to different target organs. These models are ideal for studying the mechanical mechanisms of blast injuries and provide critical theoretical foundations and design guidance for the development and optimization of blast injury protection equipment. 31 , 32 However, despite being constructed using highly realistic biomimetic materials and precise physiological anatomy, the biomechanical properties of these materials cannot fully replicate the physiological responses of real tissues. Material damage does not equate to biological tissue damage. Therefore, numerical models have inherent limitations in assessing the true physiological damage caused by blast injuries, making them unsuitable for direct use in physiological effect studies and clinical evaluations. Animal models Animal models are the most commonly used models in blast injury research. Based on the method of shock wave generation, they can be divided into free-field explosion and shock tube explosion, which are used to evaluate overpressure thresholds, injury severity, injury mechanisms, and protective strategies for blast-induced brain and lung injuries, respectively. Free-field explosion Free-field explosion models typically employ large animals for research, as their lung size and structure are similar to those of humans, making them suitable for studying blast-induced lung injuries. As early as 1968, Bowen et al. conducted free-field explosion experiments using 13 animal models, including dogs, pigs, goats, sheep, monkeys, donkeys, and cattle, to investigate the relationship between survivability and lung injury thresholds. This work led to the development of the famous Bowen curve, 33 which estimates the mortality percentage of a 70 kg male exposed to free-field shock waves, laying an important theoretical foundation for subsequent research on blast-induced lung injuries. Yelverton 34 conducted free-field explosion experiments using sheep and established a single-organ injury (IS) and systemic injury severity index (SII) scoring system. This system is an effective tool for quantifying blast-induced biological damage and is adaptable to any mammal, making it a widely applicable evaluation standard. MacFadden et al. 35 based on data from over 1,200 sheep exposed to blast injuries by the U.S. Army Medical Research and Materiel Command and the Lobdell model, 36 systematically studied the effects of free-field and confined-space (e.g., bunkers or caves) explosions on sheep lung injuries. By scaling sheep lung injury data to the 50th percentile male human data (75 kg body weight) and quantifying it as a percentage of lung injury area, the researchers used logistic analysis to construct dose-response curves for four levels of primary blast lung injury (mild, moderate, severe, and critical). Although this model achieved anthropomorphic scaling through modifications to human chest injury models and data scaling, its foundational data still originated from sheep, lacking validation with real human data, which introduces extrapolation limitations. Studies on the patterns of blast-induced lung injuries have shown that the closer the distance to the blast center, the higher the overpressure of the shock wave and the more severe the lung injury. Major pathophysiological changes include pulmonary hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, bullae, pulmonary contusion, and lung rupture. 37 , 38 , 39 Sheep models of blast-induced lung injury are also commonly used to study the molecular mechanisms of lung injury. Our team previously revealed 26 potential molecular mechanisms of blast-induced lung injury through transcriptomic sequencing analysis, 40 and identified 49 common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in lung injuries between goats and rats, 41 Among these, the IL-17 signaling pathway and vascular smooth muscle contraction pathway were identified as potential mechanisms, and five proteins—AGR2, ANKRD65, BPIFA1, BPIFB1, and KRT5—were proposed as potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets for blast-induced lung injuries. Large animals such as sheep and pigs are often used in field studies, such as weapon performance evaluations, due to their lung size and structure being similar to humans, as well as their comparable shock wave tolerance and physiological similarities. They are also suitable for long-term observation. However, their large size increases management costs and limits sample sizes, making them less suitable for laboratory-based research. Shock tube explosion Rodents, due to their ease of breeding and suitability for laboratory modeling, are the preferred animals for indoor blast injury modeling. Currently, rat and mouse models of blast injury are primarily established using two types of devices ( Figure 1 ). [FIG/TABLE OMITTED] Owing to their small size, low management costs, and ease of dissection, numerous rat and mouse models of blast-induced brain and lung injuries have been developed. These models are widely used to study overpressure thresholds, pathophysiological mechanisms, and treatment strategies for these injuries. Mishra et al. 42 and Hubbard et al. 43 established quantitative relationships between different blast overpressure (BOP) thresholds and predicted mortality rates (PMR) for brain and lung injuries in rat models ( Table 1 ). Further studies revealed significant differences in injury threshold pressures based on exposure orientation, with frontal exposure requiring a higher threshold (8 psi) compared to lateral exposure (6 psi) in the same positive phase duration of 3–5 ms. Under the same BOP, rats exposed laterally exhibited more severe pathological changes, such as pulmonary parenchymal contusion and alveolar hemorrhage, than those exposed frontally. 44 Research on BOP thresholds for brain and lung injuries in rat models is critical for guiding survival treatments and experimental designs focused on injury mechanisms. It is noteworthy that the severity of blast wave-induced injury in biological organisms is closely related to parameters such as peak overpressure, duration, and impulse, 45 with the overpressure-impulse criterion being more suitable for assessing blast-induced damage to biological targets. 46 Peak overpressure alone is only one variable. Given the physiological and mass differences across models, the duration of blast exposure must be taken into account when discussing the effects of blast exposure on different models. Therefore, total exposure dose (incorporating both overpressure and duration) should guide the design of relevant experiments. [FIG/TABLE OMITTED] Following blast-induced brain injury, mice exhibited reduced dendritic branching in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, 47 accompanied by behavioral changes such as weight loss, unstable heart rate, apnea, incontinence, motor dysfunction, and memory impairment. 48 , 49 In models of repeated blast exposure, over 21% of individuals developed spontaneous recurrent seizures. 50 , 51 These symptoms resemble mild to moderate traumatic brain injury (TBI) in humans, reliably replicating the major clinical changes associated with blast-induced neurotrauma and chronic encephalopathy. In rats with mild blast-induced brain injury, neuropathological changes, such as cerebral edema, cerebrovascular damage, and neuronal apoptosis were observed, 52 along with elevated serum levels of glial and neuronal injury markers, including S100β, MBP, and NSE. 53 At the cellular and histological levels, blast-induced lung injury in rodents is characterized by red blood cell extravasation and hemorrhagic changes in alveolar space, 54 significant infiltration of inflammatory cells (primarily neutrophils) in alveolar septa and spaces, 55 alveolar fusion due to structural destruction, and septal thickening caused by edema and fibrosis. 54 , 56 These microscopic changes collectively contribute to the characteristic histological features of blast lung injury, such as pulmonary vascular rupture, edema, and emphysema, consistent with the pathological manifestations observed in large animal models exposed to free-field explosions. Rodents, with their well-defined genetic backgrounds and advanced genetic tools, have been extensively used in molecular mechanism studies of blast-induced brain and lung injuries. Common molecular mechanisms underlying these injuries include inflammatory responses, 54 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 oxidative stress, 60 , 61 , 62 and apoptosis. 52 , 60 , 63 , 64 Additionally, rat models of blast-induced brain injury have revealed pathological responses, such as cerebral hemorrhage. 65 cerebrovascular structural damage, 66 blood-brain barrier disruption, 67 and mitochondrial dysfunction. 68 The abnormal accumulation of phosphorylated tau protein and glial cell activation in the brain following blast injury suggest the development of chronic traumatic encephalopathy. 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 Studies on signaling pathways in lung injury have identified the NF-κB and FosB pathways as key mediators of inflammation and oxidative stress in blast-induced lung injury in rats. 73 Proteomic analyses further revealed that blast lung injury involves lipid metabolism, 74 leukocyte transendothelial migration, phagocytosis, and immune-related pathological processes. 75 Zhang et al. 76 used single-cell transcriptomic sequencing to identify the PI3K-Akt and p53 pathways as critical regulators of autophagy and oxidative stress in blast-induced lung injury in rats. The potential biological mechanisms of blast-induced brain and lung injuries are highly complex, involving multiple intertwined pathological processes that collectively contribute to secondary injuries following blast exposure. Despite significant progress in understanding the molecular mechanisms of blast-induced brain and lung injuries, the translation of findings from animal models to targeted therapies for humans remains unfulfilled. In studies on protective strategies for blast-induced lung injury in mice, compounds such as tanshinone IIA, 77 chitosan oligosaccharides, 78 and hydrogen-rich water 79 have been shown to alleviate inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, thereby protecting lung function and reducing injury. Metformin has been found to mitigate blast-induced lung injury in rats, 80 while Ning et al. 81 , 82 demonstrated that 30 min of localized hypothermic treatment or 60 min of systemic hypothermia reduced pulmonary edema and significantly decreased levels of Mpo, TNF-α, and IL-6. These theoretical findings from rodent models require further validation in preclinical models. Notably, in a repetitive bTBI mouse model, proteomic analysis identified 15 high-confidence biomarkers, previously validated in TBI and epilepsy studies. Among these, four biomarkers are listed in publicly available databases, and eight are proposed to have potential associations with TBI and epilepsy. 50 This discovery significantly advances the translation of animal model findings to preclinical applications, providing a critical foundation for the validation and application of bTBI-related biomarkers. In-depth studies on the assessment, mechanisms, and protective measures for blast-induced lung injury using rodent models have provided a wealth of theoretical insights and data on its pathophysiological mechanisms. However, due to significant differences in anatomical structure, physiological function, and shock wave tolerance between rodents and humans, the direct applicability of these findings to human blast injury research requires further validation using human-derived models. Postmortem human surrogate models Postmortem human surrogate (PMHS) models are often used to assess bTBI due to their ability to provide authentic human data for BOP threshold evaluations. Bir 83 were the first to establish a blast PMHS craniocerebral model, measuring ICP in human subjects post-blast. The results were consistent with predictions from finite element models, further validating the accuracy of human data-based finite element models. Ganpule et al. 84 used PMHS heads to construct blast injury models and found that 70 kPa BOP caused no injury, 140 kPa resulted in moderate to severe injury, and 200 kPa led to severe injury. PMHS remains the gold standard in biomechanical research on blast-induced brain injuries. Salzar et al. 85 replaced the cranial contents of PMHS with a mixture of saline and ballistic gel, discovering that cavitation could occur at pressure levels below the 50% risk threshold for blast lung injury, with cranial flexion being a potential mechanism. Iwaskiw et al. 86 subjected four PMHS to short-duration dynamic overpressure and observed that peak ICP increased with higher overpressure doses, accompanied by delayed head kinematics and brain displacements of approximately 2–6 mm. These studies provide valuable data for evaluating blast-induced brain injury models. Shively et al. 87 conducted a comparative analysis of brain samples from 13 individuals with varying degrees of blast exposure and three normal human brain samples. They identified significant astrocytic scarring in the same brain regions in both acute and chronic blast exposure cases. This study is one of the few direct histopathological investigations of human brains, offering direct evidence for the mechanisms underlying chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) in post-war soldiers. PMHS models provide authentic human biomechanical response data in blast wave research and are instrumental in validating the effectiveness of other models. However, their limitations include the lack of physiological activity, making them unsuitable for medium-to long-term studies. Additionally, PMHS samples require stringent preservation conditions, are prone to degradation, and are difficult to obtain, necessitating rigorous ethical review. Therefore, PMHS models are primarily used for short-term biomechanical mechanism studies and the evaluation of protective equipment. In vitro models Although studies on the overpressure thresholds, pathophysiology, and mechanisms of blast-induced injuries primarily rely on animal models, the cellular-level mechanisms of primary blast injuries remain speculative. 88 With the development of laboratory-based shock wave generators, in vitro blast injury models have been successfully established and are increasingly used in blast injury research. These models provide valuable insights into the mechanical responses and pathological changes at the cellular level in blast-induced brain and lung injuries. Based on their complexity, in vitro models can be categorized into cell models and organoid models. Cell models Arun and his team pioneered the development of a shock tube-based neuronal cell injury model to study the effects of shock waves on mouse neuroblastoma, rat glioblastoma, and human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells. The results demonstrated significant neurobiological effects, including decreased adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels and increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. 89 Subsequently, Morrison’s team used a shock tube device to construct the first in vitro model of shock wave-induced blood-brain barrier dysfunction. 90 In 2015, Nicole E. Zander from the U.S. Army Research Laboratory developed an in vitro blast injury model using real explosives, submerging rat PC12 cells in water and exposing them to blasts. After 24 h, a significant increase in axonal varicosities was observed in damaged cells, with repeated blast exposure leading to membrane damage, increased permeability, and reduced cell viability. 91 In a co-culture of rat primary cortical neurons and glial cells exposed to blasts, increased membrane permeability and disrupted ion homeostasis were observed, suggesting that mechanical forces on the cell membrane are a major factor in cellular damage. 92 In a primary cell injury model using rat dermal papilla fibroblasts, a 127 kPa (duration<10 ms) shock wave significantly reduced cell viability within 24 h, 93 providing a reference for overpressure threshold studies. A blast injury model using human microvascular endothelial cells also revealed elevated ROS, Ca 2+ levels, and mitochondrial gene expression, along with reduced cell viability, indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction in these cells is a key process in blood-brain barrier disruption and blast-induced brain injury progression. 94 Snapper et al. 95 further co-cultured human iPSC-derived neurons, astrocytes, and microglia and exposed them to a blast simulator, observing increased LDH activity and glutamate levels, significant axonal varicosities, and the release of brain injury biomarkers, such as UCHL1, NF-H, and S100β at different time points. These studies collectively demonstrate that bTBI cell models are valuable tools for investigating cellular and molecular changes following blast exposure. In BLI research, Ding et al. 96 established a rat type II alveolar epithelial cell injury model using a shock wave physiotherapy device, which can be applied to studies on lung injuries caused by gas explosions and other blast-related forces. After exposure to shock waves, A549 alveolar epithelial cells exhibited typical pathological molecular changes associated with blast lung injury, such as increased expression of inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, reduced superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and activation of apoptotic factors (NF-κB, Bax, and caspase-3). 97 In vitro cell models, due to their high operability and stability, play a critical role in blast injury research. They not only reveal the effects of shock waves on cellular biological behavior and molecular mechanisms, particularly transcriptomic changes and epigenetic modifications, but also enhance the clinical relevance and translational potential of research findings by incorporating human-derived cells. This represents a significant advancement in blast injury research models. However, primary cells derived from normal human tissues have a limited lifespan in vitro due to the Hayflick limit, undergoing senescence and death after a finite number of passages. Additionally, cell models are limited in their ability to comprehensively assess the temporal dynamics of molecular mechanisms and drug treatment effects, which constrains their application in long-term injury effect studies and pathological process research. Organoid models Organoids are three-dimensional (3D) structures formed in vitro through the self-organization of stem cells, progenitor cells, and/or differentiated cells via cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions. They can recapitulate the structure and function of corresponding tissues or organs in vivo across multiple dimensions. 98 To date, researchers have successfully constructed organoids of major organs, including intestinal, retinal, brain, liver, lung, hippocampal, and skin organoids, using adult stem cells (ASCs) or pluripotent stem cells (PSCs). 99 Human-derived organoids are 3D cellular models generated in vitro from human tissue-derived cells, embryonic stem cells, or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). These models mimic the structure and function of human tissues and can replicate processes such as tissue development, differentiation, and maturation. 100 , 101 Huang et al. 102 used human iPSCs to generate arcuate organoids in vitro , enabling the study of cell-type-specific features and disease-related mechanisms during early human arcuate development. Giandomenico et al. 103 improved early human brain organoid construction methods, successfully cultivating more advanced human brain organoid models from human embryonic stem cells and achieving long-term culture. These models are used to study diseases and mechanisms related to later stages of neural development. The successful construction of human-derived organoids has made their application in injury assessment possible ( Figure 2 ). Ramirez et al. 104 , 105 developed an in vitro platform for human TBI using human iPSC-derived brain organoids and a controlled cortical impact (CCI) model. They found that brain organoids subjected to mechanical impact reproduced key physiological changes observed in TBI, such as neuronal damage, loss, and astrocyte proliferation. This demonstrates that brain organoid-based blast injury models provide an innovative and feasible alternative for studying blast-induced brain injuries in vitro . In 2022, Silvosa et al. 106 combined blast overpressure with human iPSC-derived organoids to create a human brain organoid model exposed to blast shock waves. They observed increased apoptosis and neurophysiological differences in brain organoids exposed to 350 kPa (duration 8 ms) overpressure, consistent with the findings of Logan et al. 93 n cell-based studies. LaPlaca and Brody 107 commented on Silvosa’s innovative approach, praising the creation of this novel in vitro brain blast injury model and describing it as a groundbreaking advancement in the field. This highlights the feasibility of applying human-derived organoid models to blast injury research. Additionally, Budeus et al. 108 combined human iPSCs with matrix-free culture techniques to construct human lung organoids in vitro , which were used to study radiation-induced lung injury. This revealed the radiation sensitivity, cellular stress responses, and injury markers of different lung cell types post-radiation. Zhou et al. 109 also reviewed the potential applications of respiratory, skin, and otolaryngological organoids, as well as corresponding organ-on-a-chip systems, in the assessment of injuries caused by biochemical weapons. Currently, the published literature on the application of organoids in blast injury research has primarily focused on blast-induced brain injuries, with no reports on the use of organoids for studying blast-induced lung injuries. This indicates that employing lung organoids for research on blast-induced lung injuries may represent a novel field that is ripe for in-depth exploration. Introducing lung organoid models of blast injury in the study of blast-induced lung injuries could potentially yield deeper insights into the mechanisms by which blast waves affect lung physiology. [FIG/TABLE OMITTED] The fused organoid system involves co-culturing organoids with different structures and functions or integrating specific cell types with organoids to create more complex and functionally advanced 3D biological models. Researchers have successfully assembled the cortex, hippocampus and thalamus organoids into more complex and advanced brain assemblies using microfluidic technology for the study of human brain development and disease treatment. 110 Sun et al. 111 co-cultured vascular and brain organoids to generate vascularized brain organoids, which not only allowed observation of brain development but also facilitated the study of interactions between vascular and neuronal cells. Future research could leverage the fused organoid system and vascularized organoid technology to construct highly biomimetic organoid models of blast injuries. Such platforms would more accurately simulate the coordinated responses of different cell types following blast exposure, aiding in the in-depth analysis of blast injury pathophysiology, uncovering cell-cell interactions, and exploring potential therapeutic targets. The breakthrough development of organoid technology has revolutionized blast injury research, enabling systematic studies on the cellular and molecular mechanisms of blast injuries and protective strategies using human-derived organoids. This provides a new research paradigm for advancing scientific understanding in related fields. Artificial intelligence models Artificial intelligence (AI) models represent an advanced analytical approach based on machine learning and deep learning technologies, which can be utilized to systematically assess and predict the injury effects of blast shock waves on the human body. These models integrate multi-source data, including experimental animal studies, clinical cases, and literature reports, and employ algorithms such as logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), and support vector machines (SVM) to achieve precise identification of blast injury types, quantitative prediction of injury severity and thresholds, and intelligent assessment of injury risks ( Figure 3 ). [FIG/TABLE OMITTED] In blast injury identification research, Harrington et al. 112 successfully constructed a diagnostic model for chronic blast-related mild traumatic brain injury (bmTBI) using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and the SVM method from machine learning. The study found that the bmTBI group exhibited significant abnormalities in radial diffusivity (RD), fractional anisotropy (FA), and the axial/radial diffusivity ratio (AD/RD) across multiple white matter tracts. By combining cross-validation methods, the diagnostic model identified the most discriminative DTI feature combinations, achieving an accuracy of 89% in distinguishing bmTBI patients from healthy controls, with sensitivity and specificity of 90% and 88%, respectively. These results demonstrate that the SVM model incorporating multiple DTI metrics can provide a reliable basis for diagnosing blast-induced brain injuries. In the prediction of injury thresholds, Boutillier et al. 113 analyzed lung injury data from large mammals and experimental chest response data from pigs exposed to blast shock waves. Using binary logistic regression analysis, they assessed the tolerance limits for blast-induced lung injuries. The results showed that for a 50 kg pig exposed laterally to a blast shock wave near a wall, the incident impulse threshold for lung injury was 58.3 kPa·ms, while the threshold for severe lung injury was 232.8 kPa·ms. The study also proposed methods to correlate these tolerance limits with human model responses to evaluate the effectiveness of chest protection systems against blast threats. AI models can also be applied to the intelligent assessment of injury risks. Cavitation, considered one of the mechanisms of blast-induced brain injury, was studied using k-NN and SVM algorithms to predict the number of cavitation bubbles generated at different temperatures with high accuracy (up to 90%). This provides a new tool for investigating the mechanisms of blast-induced brain injuries. 114 Walilko et al. 115 developed a predictive model for blast-induced neurological injury risks using machine learning methods and experimental data from 71 Yucatan minipigs. The results indicated that even low-level blast exposure could trigger glial cell activation, inflammation, and neurodegenerative responses. A single exposure to 100 kPa caused significant neurological functional impairment, while the injury threshold decreased with repeated exposures. These findings demonstrate that AI models can effectively identify and predict the risks of neurological functional damage caused by blast exposure, providing support for the development of evidence-based blast exposure guidelines. AI models demonstrate significant strengths in blast injury evaluation by leveraging accumulated complex multi-modal datasets (e.g., multi-omics profiles, high-resolution imaging, and dynamic physiological signals) to identify latent correlations within these data. This capability enables the discovery of pathological patterns and biomarkers undetectable through conventional statistical approaches, thereby empowering clinicians to rapidly discern injury phenotypes and assess trauma severity with enhanced triage and diagnostic efficiency. However, the application of this technology relies heavily on large amounts of high-quality experimental data as a training foundation. Therefore, the predictive accuracy and generalization capabilities of AI models in blast injury research require continuous optimization and validation to enhance their performance. However, the translational implementation of this technology depends on extensive high-fidelity empirical experimental datasets as foundational training inputs. Through systematic extraction of latent critical features from multimodal data streams, it establishes robust correlations between blast wave parameters (e.g., peak overpressure, impulse duration) and injury phenotypes—including damage classification, pathophysiological effects, and severity gradation—to enable predictive modeling and clinical assessment. Additionally, the lifelong learning capabilities must also be incorporated to ensure that AI models can proactively read and update their knowledge base whenever new blast injury data are inputted, thereby continuously enhancing the accuracy of injury assessment through adaptive learning mechanisms. Another notable advantage of AI models in blast injury research lies in their potential to address cross-species data transfer challenges through transfer learning. Given the scarcity of publicly available clinical datasets on human blast injuries, current models predominantly rely on animal experimental data, resulting in limited clinical applicability. Transfer learning may effectively mitigate this constraint by extracting cross-species shared imaging features (e.g., edge characteristics, texture patterns, and morphological properties of injured tissues). Specifically, the model can first undergo pre-training on extensive animal data to learn general blast injury feature representations, followed by fine-tuning with limited human-specific data to achieve cross-species adaptation. This approach not only reduces dependence on human data but also significantly enhances the AI model’s analytical capability for clinical imaging, providing a viable technical pathway for intelligent blast injury diagnosis. Conclusions and future prospects Craniocerebral and pulmonary injuries are the main types of blast injuries. The development and application of accurate evaluation models is very important for the analysis of injury mechanism, optimization of protection strategies, and clinical treatment. Traditional models, such as animal models, numerical simulation models and cadaveric models, have played an important role in revealing the biomechanical response, overpressure threshold and pathophysiological mechanism of blast injury, but there are still significant limitations. Recent years, the development of extracorporeal shock wave cell model and organoid model has opened up a new direction for the study of blast injury. The human-derived cell model effectively solves the problems of species difference and genetic background inconsistency in animal experiments, and the model has high stability and strong operability, marking an important progress in the construction of blast injury model. With its highly bionic three-dimensional structure and function, human-derived organoids significantly improve the physiological relevance of models, especially in the analysis of cellular and molecular mechanisms and drug screening. At the same time, artificial intelligence models based on machine learning and deep learning realize the intelligence of injury pattern recognition, threshold prediction and risk assessment by integrating multi-source data, which greatly improves the research efficiency and clinical translation potential. However, the application of in vitro techniques to the study of blast shock waves is still in the early exploratory stage. Although previous studies have successfully constructed in vitro models of shock waves acting on cells or organoids, there is a lack of accurate measurement data of the surface pressure value of shock waves acting on cells or organoids. There is still a lack of grading basis and standard for the degree of damage to cells or organoids caused by shock waves. Future research needs to focus on the following directions: developing grading standards for the degree of blast wave damage to cells and organoids, integrating a variety of pressure measurement techniques to obtain shock wave overpressure values, and establishing the corresponding relationship between mechanical parameters and damage degree. Optimizing organoid technology, improving its complexity and functionality through vascularization, multi-organ co-culture and dynamic mechanical loading, and accurately simulating the molecular pathological process of blast injury. Multi-model fusion technology is developed to combine the mechanical analysis of numerical simulation, the physiological simulation of organoids, and the data-driven of artificial intelligence to construct a multi-scale and multi-dimensional comprehensive evaluation system to make up for the shortcomings of a single model. Finally, by promoting the deep integration of basic research and clinical needs, accelerating the development of targeted therapy strategies and the formulation of protection standards, it will provide theoretical basis and technical support for the accurate evaluation and efficient treatment of blast injury.
