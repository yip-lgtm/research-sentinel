Abstract

Objective:

To define Military Occupational Blast Exposure (MOBE) as a standardized construct of cumulative occupational blast exposure spanning low-level blast and high-level blast, and propose reproducible MOBE risk classification groups based on military occupations when objective blast records or detailed lifetime interviews are unavailable.

Setting:

Data were drawn from 2 Long-term Impact of Military-relevant Brain Injury Consortium–Chronic Effects of Neurotrauma Consortium (LIMBIC-CENC) studies: a retrospective cohort study and a multisite prospective longitudinal cohort.

Participants:

The Phenotype Study included 2 522 880 post-9/11 service members and Veterans. The Prospective Longitudinal Study (PLS) included 1666 service members and Veterans who had experienced combat.

Design:

The Phenotype Study was used to operationalize MOBE risk classification and characterize demographic and military correlates. PLS data were used to evaluate whether MOBE risk classification aligned with blast exposure history and blast traumatic brain injury (TBI) history.

Main Measures:

MOBE risk classification was compared with the generalized blast exposure value (GBEV) and blast exposure category scores from the Blast Exposure Threshold Survey, as well as blast TBI history from clinical interview data.

Results:

The Phenotype study classified 31.1% of military occupational codes into high-risk MOBE (MOBE+). In the PLS, MOBE+ individuals were more likely than MOBE− individuals to report blast TBI history (43.7% vs. 27.0%) and exceed the GBEV threshold of 200 000 (66.0% vs. 42.2%; both P < .001). MOBE+ individuals also reported higher mean blast exposure scores across all categories, with significant differences for small arms and large explosives. Occupational-level analyses showed meaningful heterogeneity in blast exposure profiles across high-risk occupations.

Conclusions:

MOBE risk classification differentiated meaningful patterns of blast exposure and blast-related injury in a well-characterized cohort. Findings support MOBE as a practical, scalable, standardized approach for estimating occupational blast exposure risk and improving comparability across studies when detailed individual exposure histories are unavailable.

Keywords: blast, military, neurotrauma, traumatic brain injury, Veteran

Military personnel are routinely exposed to a range of blast forces across training and operational settings. High-level blast (HLB) exposures (eg, improvised explosive devices, incoming munitions) have been extensively studied, but low-level blast (LLB) exposures are far more common, often repetitive, and occupationally driven.1 These exposures are a product of routine activities such as breaching, artillery, mortar, and shoulder-mounted weapon firing, and explosive ordnance disposal. Although typically subconcussive, accumulating evidence suggests that repeated LLB exposure over a career may contribute to cumulative effects on brain health, cognition, and long-term functioning.2–6

Measurement of blast exposure remains a central challenge in military and Veteran research.7,8 Direct recording of exposures using blast gauges or wearable sensors provides objective data on acute exposures but is limited to small, time-bounded samples and cannot characterize exposure over a military career.7 Structured interviews (eg, Salisbury Blast Interview, SBI9) provide detailed characterization of lifetime blast exposure, including contextual, experiential, and event-specific features, but are labor-intensive and difficult to administer in large samples. Self-report measures (eg, Blast Exposure Threshold Survey [BETS]10; Blast Ordnance and Occupational exposure Measure11) require less administration time and can also capture important characteristics of exposure history, but they differ in the dimensions emphasized and in how data are summarized. More broadly, the field also relies on overlapping terminology that often conflates exposure, injury mechanism, and clinical outcome.

Importantly, challenges in this area extend beyond measurement tools alone. The field has historically relied on a range of overlapping and sometimes inconsistent conceptual definitions to characterize blast exposure and its potential consequences. Terms such as LLB, HLB, subconcussive blast exposure, blast traumatic brain injury (blast TBI), and blast-induced neurotrauma (BINT) are often used across disciplines, yet these terms reflect different underlying constructs, including exposure type, injury mechanism, or clinical outcome (Table 1). These distinctions are meaningful, but they are not used consistently across studies. In many cases, definitions are anchored to injury outcomes, although most LLB exposures during training and operational activities do not result in clinically identifiable neurotrauma at the time of exposure. Differences in measurement and definitions complicate quantification of cumulative blast exposure and limit comparison across cohorts and research designs.

Recent epidemiological work has relied on Military Occupational Specialty (MOS) as a proxy for occupational blast exposure risk.2,5,12 This approach leverages the predictable patterns of weapon system use inherent to certain occupations, allowing for indirect estimation of cumulative exposure when direct measurement is not feasible. High-risk occupations such as artillery, explosive ordnance disposal, and breaching units involve repeated exposure to blast-producing weapon systems, whereas administrative, medical, or support roles typically involve minimal exposure. Prior work suggests that occupation-based classification captures meaningful variation in blast exposure risk and can be linked to acute and long-term health outcomes in large data sets.2,5,12–15 Although it cannot replace individual-level exposure measures, it provides a complementary approach for population-based research.

This article has 3 objectives. First, to improve conceptual and methodological clarity in research on blast exposure, we formally introduce and define the term Military Occupational Blast Exposure (MOBE) within the context of existing definitions related to blast exposure (Table 1; Figure 1). Second, to enhance reproducibility and generalizability in the field, we define cumulative MOBE across a career as the construct of interest and describe a risk classification procedure that operationalizes this construct in large data sets. Using data derived from military occupational codes, we demonstrate the distribution of MOBE risk groups and characterize demographic and occupational differences. Third, we validate these risk classifications using individual-level exposure history data from a well-characterized sample of service members and Veterans.

MILITARY OCCUPATIONAL BLAST EXPOSURE TERM AND CONSTRUCT

MOBE is the term used to describe any exposure to blast overpressure, impulse, and other forces resulting from military occupational activities and environments. It encompasses both high-level and LLB exposures. In this article, the construct of interest is cumulative MOBE across a military career, including exposures arising from occupational duties, weapon system use, and operational settings. Although MOBE may be used to refer to a single exposure event, the construct as defined here refers to the accumulation of such exposures over time during military service.

MOBE risk classification

To operationalize this construct in large data sets, military occupational codes can be used to classify service members into higher-risk (MOBE+) and lower-risk (MOBE−) categories. These categories are based on the relative frequency, intensity, and proximity of blast-producing activities characteristic of each occupation. In this application, MOBE risk classification functions as a proxy measure for cumulative occupational blast exposure, enabling consistent and scalable estimation of blast exposure burden in large administrative or epidemiological data sets. This approach extends prior MOS-based studies by applying occupational codes across service branches in a consistent manner to classify relative exposure risk. The specific procedures used in this study are described in the Methods.

METHODS

Study design, setting, and participants

Data were sourced from 2 Long-term Impact of Military-relevant Brain Injury Consortium-Chronic Effects of Neurotrauma Consortium (LIMBIC-CENC) projects: The Phenotype study (retrospective cohort) and the Prospective Longitudinal Study (PLS). The phenotype study addresses the second aim of this article, whereas the PLS study addresses the third aim.

Phenotype study (MOBE risk classification procedure)

This cohort comprises post-9/11 Veterans who received care in the Military Health System (MHS) at least once a year for 3 or more years between 1999 and 2019. For those who entered care in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), we required 2 years of VHA care (2000-2019). This work was approved as an exempt study by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Department of Defense (DoD) Human Research Protection Office.

Participant selection for both the Phenotype and Prospective Longitudinal Studies is presented in Figure 2. N = 2 530 847 individuals who served after September 11, 2001, were identified using the VA DoD Identity Repository (VADIR). The primary exclusion was documented DoD care for fewer than 3 years during the study period. Among individuals with 3 or more years of DoD care, we excluded individuals who/se: a) care in the DoD occurred after retirement; b) last military episode before retirement ended before September 12, 2001; c) had no valid occupational data during the study period; or d) had a documented termination date before the personnel start date. We also excluded individuals who were 17 or 69 years or older at separation.

A total of n = 7967 individuals were excluded based on the criteria described above, leaving an analytic cohort of N = 2 522 880. For these individuals, we compiled health system data from the DoD, including the DoD Trauma Registry (focused on TBI exposure) and Theatre Data Management Store (TMDS), using the DoD and VA Infrastructure for Clinical Intelligence.

MOBE risk classification procedure

Supplemental Table A, http://links.lww.com/JHTR/B95 summarizes details about job classification. High-risk MOBE occupations were identified based on input from a workgroup comprising clinical and operational members from the United States Departments of Defense and Veterans Affairs, which focused on examining high-risk MOBE. This classification was informed by prior occupation-based blast exposure research and the expected frequency, intensity, and proximity of blast-producing occupational activities across military roles. Occupations were classified as higher risk when the occupational duties commonly involved repeated exposure to blast-producing weapons systems or environments, such as artillery, explosive ordnance disposal, breaching, infantry, armor, and other combat- or weapons-focused roles. Candidate occupations and corresponding occupational code groupings were reviewed by the investigative team for consistency with the MOBE construct, prior literature, and available military occupational coding structures.

Using this approach, we identified the specific high-risk MOBE occupations across all periods of military service as: Special Forces; Ground Naval Arms; Military Trainer; Rocket Artillery; Artillery Gunnery; Missile Artillery; Combat Engineer; Armor Amphibious; Infantry; and Military Police. Individuals with 2 or more of these high-risk occupations over the study period were classified as Multiple-Military Occupational Category at High Risk for MOBE (Multiple-MOC), allowing for an understanding of potential additive or multiplicative effects relative to single occupations. Service members were also dichotomized into high-(MOBE+) or low-risk (MOBE−) MOBE occupations. Total time spent in each high-risk MOBE occupation group, and the time each service member served in their designated high-risk occupation, were also calculated.

Demographic and military characteristics

Demographic and military characteristics were collected from VADIR and reflect the time at military separation. Demographic variables included age at separation, biological sex, race and ethnicity [White (Hispanic and non-Hispanic), Black (Hispanic and non-Hispanic), Asian American/Pacific Islander, Native American, unknown], and education (high school, some college, college graduate, graduate school, unknown). Military characteristics included the highest rank on file (enlisted, officer/warrant officer), the most recent military component (Active Duty, National Guard, Reserve), branch (Army, Marines, Navy, Air Force, Space Force, Coast Guard, and other), and total years of service.

Prospective longitudinal study (validation of risk classification levels)

The LIMBIC-CENC PLS is a multisite longitudinal observational study with a cohort of service members and Veterans who deployed in support of combat operations. The recruitment process and methods of the PLS have previously been detailed.16–18 Study procedures are overseen and approved by the respective IRB at each site. Data from this study were used to demonstrate actual individual-reported blast exposure for MOBE risk classification.

Data for the present analyses were from the N = 3011 participants who completed an in-person assessment between January 2015–June 2025. Individuals missing military occupational data were removed from this sample, n = 416. The BETS10 was incorporated into the PLS battery several years after study initiation and, therefore, was not administered to all participants, which accounts for the remaining n = 929 participants excluded. The final sample size was N = 1666.

Risk classification

The Phenotype study used VADIR records in the second step of the MOBE classification; however, the PLS cohort lacked access to this data source. Therefore, MOBE risk classification for the PLS was limited to DoD occupation codes available in the Defense Enrollment Eligibility Reporting System data within the DoD and VA Informatics and Computing Structure.

Blast exposure threshold survey

The BETS10 quantified MOBE at the individual level. The BETS is a self-report questionnaire that estimates the frequency of exposure to 5 weapons categories: small/medium arms, large/shoulder-fired arms, artillery/vehicle-mounted, small explosives/grenades, and large explosives/Improvised Explosive Devices (IEDs). A generalized blast exposure value (GBEV) is calculated as a cumulative, weighted index of blast exposure across all categories. Although the BETS includes some events that may reflect higher-level exposures, it is primarily intended as an estimate of MOBE. A GBEV score of 200 000 is the threshold recognized for which individuals are likely to report more severe neurobehavioral symptoms,10 which has been previously identified as statistically appropriate due to the non-normal distribution in the PLS sample.19

The GBEV is calculated using a formula estimating lifetime exposure frequency across 5 Blast Exposure Categories (BEC), including 1) small and medium arms (eg, hand-held firearms and rifles); 2) large arms (eg, shoulder-fired or carried on a person, rocket-propelled weapons systems, recoilless rifles, and launchers); 3) artillery, missile weapon systems, and large arms mounted on vehicles, aircraft, or boats; 4) small explosives or grenades; and 5) large or targeted explosives (eg, breaching explosives, disposal explosives, and heavy charges). The score for each BEC is estimated by multiplying exposure years, months, days, and exposures per day together. Calculations are addressed in detail by Modica et al,10 (2021).

Data analysis

Phenotype study

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the full sample and by MOBE risk group. Age and other time-related variables (eg, years of service, years in occupation) are reported as median (Mdn) and interquartile range (IQR) because their distributions were highly skewed in this relatively young cohort. Group differences in demographic and military characteristics between MOBE− and MOBE+ groups were evaluated using chi-square tests for categorical variables and tests of medians for continuous variables. Effect sizes are reported as Cramer V (V) for chi-square tests and nonparametric Cohen d (d) for tests of medians.

Prospective longitudinal study

Pairwise comparisons between MOBE risk groups were conducted for GBEV and all BEC scores using independent samples t-tests. MOBE risk classification for this sample, as well as calculation of GBEV and BEC scores, is described above. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for time in service were also conducted for GBEV and BEC comparisons. Because MOBE+ and MOBE− groups did not significantly differ by time in service (P = .450), and adjustment for time in service did not meaningfully change the findings, unadjusted results are presented in the main text.

RESULTS

Phenotype study (MOBE risk classification procedure)

Characteristics of the Phenotype Study sample (N = 2 523 253) were broadly consistent with the military population (Table 2). The median age at military separation was 31 years (IQR = 63), and most (82.5%) were male, White, non-Hispanic (62.8%) with a high school diploma or GED as the highest education attained (69.2%). Most individuals in this sample were Enlisted (86.4%), active duty (48.3%), and in the Army (48.2%) with 10.2 (IQR = 59.7) total years of service.

DISCUSSION

This study makes both a conceptual and a methodological contribution to military and Veteran blast research. Conceptually, it defines MOBE and identifies cumulative MOBE across a career as the construct of interest. Methodologically, it presents and evaluates a reproducible occupational risk-classification approach for operationalizing that construct in large data sets. This approach meaningfully differentiated patterns of blast exposure in both large administrative data and a well-characterized prospective cohort. These findings provide conceptual clarity and empirical support for using MOBE terminology and its associated occupational risk metric in research contexts where detailed individual blast histories are unavailable or impractical to obtain.

Importantly, MOBE risk classification is independent of TBI or resulting injuries. This is consistent with established blast measurement approaches that assess exposure independent from injury.9–11 Although blast can be a mechanism of TBI, most exposures in training and operational environments do not result in symptoms of concussion.8,9 Treating occupational blast exposure as a TBI-agnostic construct allows examination of cumulative effects that may accrue across a career, including effects that occur without an acute TBI diagnosis.

Analyses supported this MOBE operationalization. Specifically, individuals in occupations with high MOBE risk were significantly more likely to have blast-related TBI and exceed the GBEV threshold. Although continuous GBEV scores did not significantly differ between the MOBE+ and MOBE− group, the higher mean in the MOBE+ group and substantial variability in the MOBE− group suggest that lower-risk occupations remain heterogeneous about blast exposure. This interpretation is consistent with prior validation work13 and likely reflects the characteristics of the LIMBIC-CENC PLS cohort,16,17 which includes service members and Veterans who deployed and reported combat exposure; therefore, they had substantial opportunity for exposure regardless of occupation. Accordingly, MOBE− should be interpreted as a lower occupational risk group rather than an unexposed group.

Individuals in occupations at high risk for MOBE reported higher mean scores across all BETS blast exposure categories, with significant differences for BEC 1 and BEC 5. This is consistent with the intended purpose of MOBE classification, which is to capture broad occupational blast exposure risk, rather than to distinguish cleanly among specific blast exposure types. Occupation-level analyses also indicated that high-risk occupations are not interchangeable and may have distinct exposure profiles, underscoring the value of combining broad risk classification with more detailed individual-level measures when feasible.

These findings align with prior work2,13,14 demonstrating that military occupation is consistently associated with elevated levels of blast exposure and increased likelihood of blast-related TBI, concurrently reflecting substantial variability across occupational risk levels. At the same time, they highlight an important limitation of occupation-based classification. Specifically, it identifies elevated exposure risk at the group level, but does not distinguish exposed from unexposed individuals. From an applied perspective, the pattern of exposure across these groups does not necessarily imply that any individual’s exposure will lead to problematic outcomes, underscoring the need for complementary individual-level measures when precision estimation of blast dose or exposure history is required. However, MOBE risk classification can be used when more precise estimates of a specific individual’s exposure are unavailable.

Because feasible, standardized methods for estimating blast exposure across study designs remain limited,8,20 MOBE risk classification can address an important measurement gap. The current findings, together with previous validation studies, indicate that occupational blast-risk categories correspond to individually assessed blast exposure histories.13 This simple classification system supports harmonization across study designs and sizes, improves interpretability of findings across active duty and Veteran research, and enhances our ability to compare outcomes across samples when objective measurements, records, or detailed lifetime interviews are not available.

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be considered. First, MOBE risk classification is derived from occupational categories and does not capture individual-level blast characteristics such as peak overpressure, impulse duration, timing, or exposure frequency. Accordingly, it reflects occupational blast exposure potential rather than a precise estimate of cumulative blast dose. Although meaningful differences were observed across occupational groups, heterogeneity within groups is expected because weapon systems, training tempo, deployment context, and protective practices vary across units and career paths.

Second, the operationalization of MOBE risk classification differed modestly between the Phenotype Study and the PLS because of differences in available occupational data. The more comprehensive coding available in the Phenotype Study identified approximately 1.4% additional individuals who served in occupations at high risk for MOBE who would have been classified into the MOBE− group in the PLS. Although this likely contributed to heterogeneity in the PLS MOBE− group, the overall consistency of findings across studies suggests that the classification is reasonably robust to modest implementation differences.

Third, these analyses were descriptive and demonstrative and did not adjust for demographic or military covariates that may influence both occupational assignment and blast exposure opportunity. In addition, diagnostic and administrative data are subject to variability in health care access, documentation practices, and underreporting, particularly for mild TBI and symptoms that may not come to clinical attention. Because the Phenotype Study relied on Defense Health Agency data and the PLS included mostly Veterans, findings may also reflect differences in documentation context across samples. Finally, these retrospective analyses cannot establish temporal ordering of exposure and outcome.

CONCLUSION

This study establishes MOBE as a term and identifies cumulative MOBE across a career as the construct of interest, further presenting a practical metric for estimating exposure risk in large data sets such as electronic health records. By providing common terminology and a standardized method for estimating occupational blast exposure risk, this approach may improve comparability across studies, strengthen synthesis across cohorts and eras of service, and support investigation of cumulative effects that extend beyond combat exposure and associated conditions such as TBI and PTSD. In Veteran-focused research, MOBE risk classification may be especially useful for examining long-term trajectories, including neurological, cognitive, sensory, psychological, psychosocial, and broader physical health outcomes, as well as diagnoses, disability, health care utilization, and mortality, when individual exposure histories are unavailable. More broadly, applying a consistent definition and measurement approach across diverse samples and study designs may help identify at-risk occupational groups, inform surveillance and prevention strategies, and support development of targeted interventions aimed at preserving brain and behavioral health throughout and beyond military service.
