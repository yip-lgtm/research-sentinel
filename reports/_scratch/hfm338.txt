         The NATO Science and Technology Organization
Science & Technology (S&T) in the NATO context is defined as the selective and rigorous generation and application
of state-of-the-art, validated knowledge for defence and security purposes. S&T activities embrace scientific research,
technology development, transition, application and field-testing, experimentation and a range of related scientific
activities that include systems engineering, operational research and analysis, synthesis, integration and validation
of knowledge derived through the scientific method.
In NATO, S&T is addressed using different business models, namely a collaborative business model where NATO
provides a forum where NATO Nations and partner Nations elect to use their national resources to define, conduct and
promote cooperative research and information exchange, and secondly an in-house delivery business model where S&T
activities are conducted in a NATO dedicated executive body, having its own personnel, capabilities and infrastructure.
The mission of the NATO Science & Technology Organization (STO) is to help position the Nations’ and NATO’s S&T
investments as a strategic enabler of the knowledge and technology advantage for the defence and security posture
of NATO Nations and partner Nations, by conducting and promoting S&T activities that augment and leverage
the capabilities and programmes of the Alliance, of the NATO Nations and the partner Nations, in support of NATO’s
objectives, and contributing to NATO’s ability to enable and influence security and defence related capability
development and threat mitigation in NATO Nations and partner Nations, in accordance with NATO policies.
The total spectrum of this collaborative effort is addressed by seven Technical Panels who manage a wide range
of scientific research activities, a Group specialising in modelling and simulation, plus a Committee dedicated
to supporting the information management needs of the organization.
     •     AVT      Applied Vehicle Technology Panel
     •     HFM      Human Factors and Medicine Panel
     •     IST      Information Systems Technology Panel
     •     NMSG NATO Modelling and Simulation Group
     •     SAS      System Analysis and Studies Panel
     •     SCI      Systems Concepts and Integration Panel
     •     SET      Sensing Technology Panel
     •     TSI      Technology and Science Incubation Panel
These Panels and Group are the power-house of the collaborative model and are made up of national representatives
as well as recognised world-class scientists, engineers and information specialists. In addition to providing critical
technical oversight, they also provide a communication link to military users and other NATO bodies.
The scientific and technological work is carried out by Technical Teams, created under one or more of these eight
bodies, for specific research activities which have a defined duration. These research activities can take a variety
of forms, including Task Groups, Workshops, Symposia, Specialists’ Meetings, Lecture Series and Technical Courses.

                                        Glossary

Biofidelic Surrogate          A physical or computational model designed to accurately replicate
                              the anatomical and physiological responses of the human body to blast
                              forces.

Blast Event                   A scenario in which a rapid release of energy (for example
                              from an explosive detonation or firing of weapons) results
                              in the generation of a blast wave [1].

Blast Exposure                Blast exposure occurs when a shockwave interacts with a person’s body.

Single Blast Exposure         Exposure to an isolated blast event.

Repetitive Blast Exposure     Exposure to more than one blast event.

Cumulative Blast Exposure     The sum of exposures/events that occurred over a defined period.

Lifetime Blast Exposure       Cumulative exposure over an individual’s life.

High-Level Blast (HLB)        Blast exposure generally experienced in combat settings as a result
Exposure                      of incoming or enemy-inflicted munitions, such as IEDs,
                              rocket-propelled munitions, etc. [2].

Low-Level Blast (LLB)         Blast exposure generally but not exclusively occurring within
Exposure                      operational and training environments from outgoing (user directed)
                              munitions [2].

Occupational Blast Exposure   Blast exposures experienced in the course of performing one’s job [2].

Blast Injury                  Direct tissue damage and its downstream effects resulting from a blast
                              overpressure wave coupling into the body. High-level blast
                              and low-level blast may both be associated with blast injury. Low-level
                              blast may be associated with sub-clinical injury that may result
                              in cumulative, clinically apparent symptoms over time [3], [4], [5], [6],
                              [7]. Types of blast injury are listed in Table 2-1.

Blast Overpressure (BOP)      The pressure above ambient atmospheric pressure. Units
                              for overpressure are the same as for pressure. Overpressure is sometimes
                              called gauge pressure or static pressure [8].

Peak Overpressure             The maximum value of overpressure at a given location,
                              which is generally experienced at the instant the shock (or blast) wave
                              reaches that location [1].

On-Body Overpressure          Overpressure at a particular point on the body, also known as body load.




STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                           xi
 Incident Overpressure    Used here to describe the blast overpressure that may be recorded in free
                          space, such that its recording does not perturb the blast field. This
                          overpressure is normally approximated in explosive trials by the use of
                          streamlined pressure sensors [1].

 Reflected Overpressure   The pressure caused by the reflection of a shock wave from a surface
                          or interface (change in medium). This pressure is maximum
                          when the incident shock velocity is perpendicular to a non-responding
                          surface but is not a monotonic function of the incident angle [1].

 Blast Wave               A shock wave which decays immediately after the peak is reached. This
                          decay occurs in all variables, including pressure, density, and material
                          velocity. The rate of decay is, in general, different for each of the
                          parameters [8].

 Contextual Information   Information about the blast exposure event, environment, and personnel
                          involved.

 Health Hazard            An existing or likely condition inherent to the operation or use
                          of materiel that can cause personnel death, injury, illness, disability,
                          and/or reduced job performance. It is important to distinguish between
                          hazards inherent in the normal use and maintenance tasks and those
                          hazards related to equipment failures, mishaps, or human errors [9].

 Health Surveillance      The regular or repeated collection, analysis, and interpretation of health-
                          related data and the dissemination of information to monitor the health of
                          a population and to identify potential risks to health, thereby enabling
                          timely interventions to prevent, treat, or control disease and injury;
                          includes occupational and environmental health surveillance [10].

 Overpressure Impulse     A parameter that indicates a measure of the load in a blast wave.
                          It is defined as the area under the pressure (expressed as force per area)
                          vs. time function [1].

 Positive Impulse         The integral of the pressure–time trace during the positive phase over
                          a selected waveform duration.

 Total Positive Impulse   The overpressure impulse of the positive phase of the overpressure over
                          the entire waveform duration from a single blast exposure. It can include
                          multiple peaks from reflected waves when present. Equation:

Cumulative Impulse                  A measure of load from a blast wave experienced by an individual over a
                                    defined period from multiple exposure events. It is calculated
                                    by summing the Positive Impulse values or the Total Positive Impulse
                                    values. These impulse values may be determined from a single sensor or
                                    when multiple sensors are being used by taking the maximum
                                    of the impulse values from these sensors.

Risk Level                          The characterisation of risk as either High, Serious, Medium, or Low [11].

Warfighter Brain Health             The physical, psychological, and cognitive status that affect
                                    a warfighter’s capacity to function adaptively in any environment
                                    and affects readiness, operational capability, mission effectiveness,
                                    and the goal to achieve overmatch or superior lethality [12].

                                   Executive Summary
Blast and the resultant overpressure (Blast Overpressure – BOP) exposure can cause negative effects
to Force Health and Readiness. Such exposures can pose a significant occupational risk among military
members both from enemy action and during training from the operation of artillery, shoulder-launched
munitions, mortars, high-calibre weapons, and explosives. Most blast exposures over a career are usually
experienced in training. Repeated exposure to Low-Level Blast (LLB) can be detrimental to brain health.
The cumulative effects of LLB exposure on brain health is of concern. This is distinct from the acute brain
effects observed with exposure to a single, high-level blast. Monitoring and documenting Blast Overpressure
(BOP) exposure is critical to better understanding the effect on brain health, and to best protect troops
throughout and beyond their military careers.

Concerned NATO allies formed a multidisciplinary team of military and civilian specialists
within the biomechanical and biomedical fields, as well as from the fields of military occupational exposure
and implementation domains to develop ‘Guidelines to Mitigate Military Occupational Brain Health Risks
from Repetitive Blast Exposure’ as part of NATO STO-TR-HFM-338 Human Factors and Medicine (HFM).
The objective of this team was to identify the best practices as well as produce knowledge to control,
minimise, and mitigate the risk of developing brain-related health problems due to blast exposure.

The HFM-338 aims to achieve this through the development of three products:
    1) Blast Interim Exposure Guidelines that may be used today to mitigate the risk of adverse brain
       health effects;
    2) Operational Guidelines that detail the development of four capabilities including: 1) blast exposure
       monitoring; 2) capture of health; 3) performance information; and 4) data; analysis of blast
       exposure, health and performance effects, mitigations and health management strategies;
    3) Research and Development Guidelines that identify critical research focus areas which, once
       addressed, will help us better monitor and understand warfighter brain health.

Warfighter brain health in this report is defined as the physical, psychological, neurological, and cognitive
status that affect a warfighter’s capacity to function adaptively in any environment and affects readiness,
operational capability, and mission effectiveness. The goal is to achieve overmatch or superior lethality
by maximising brain health. The three products were designed to offer a comprehensive approach
to implementing actions now while continuing to investigate key and targeted research areas that support
a better understanding of blast exposures and brain health effects. However, each product can be used
as a standalone tool to help drive key capabilities.
    Product 1: Blast Interim Exposure Guidelines provide interim guidance for blast exposure threshold
    values to mitigate the risk of adverse brain health effects. The Guidelines include a description
    of the state of knowledge regarding blast overpressure exposure threshold values, which can be used
    to inform occupational exposure limits. The product also includes a review of the interim brain health
    protection criteria and peak overpressure methods, with a focus on level of evidence, applicability
    for short and long-term brain health effects, and usability. This product shows the current state of science

1.1 JUSTIFICATION
Blast exposure is an important concern for military personnel due to its potential to cause significant harm.
Military personnel are at risk of experiencing blast exposure due to the nature of their work, such as during live
fire training or involvement in operations [1]. This includes exposure to explosions, heavy weapons firing,
and other types of training, as well as combat-related hazards.

It is important to note that the effects of blast exposure can be cumulative, meaning that repeated exposure
to Low-Level Blast (LLB) over time may result in long-term adverse brain health outcomes [2]
even if no immediate injury is visible. Single Blast (HLB) exposures can also inflict adverse brain health
outcomes. Both HLB and repetitive LLB brain health outcomes are thought to be a significant risk factor
for military personnel who are regularly exposed to blast events [2].

Long-term adverse brain health outcomes can lead to a range of cognitive and behavioural problems, including
memory loss, difficulty concentrating, mood changes, and mental health disorders [3], [4]. These symptoms
can significantly affect the ability of military personnel to perform their duties effectively and can negatively
affect the warfighter’s home life and life after service. Additionally, changes in brain structure and function have
been observed alongside alterations in brain biomarkers in blast exposed personnel [5], [6], [7], [8]. The risk
of injury from repetitive blast exposure may also be influenced by several individual demographic
and anthropometric factors, and pre-existing medical conditions.

Given the risks associated with blast exposure, it is essential that military personnel take steps to protect
themselves whenever possible, including the use of protective gear, training on blast safety, and minimising
exposure to potential hazards. Monitoring the blast exposure as well as the status of brain health with validated
methods is vital to mitigate the risk of development of long-term injury as well as providing important research
data to study the long-term health effects, understand dose–response for repetitive exposures over time,
and to develop strategies to mitigate these effects.

This topic requires a multidisciplinary approach and spans from threat to long-term health consequences, physics
and biomechanics of the actual physical exposure to blast load, Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)/brain health
diagnostics, epidemiology, and knowledge of military operational procedures. Furthermore, it is a problem
which extends NATO-wide, since there is a similarity in blast exposures from weapon training across countries.
However, there are large differences in attention paid to this topic across countries. As such, it is a relevant
research topic for the NATO Human Factors and Medicine panel.

1.3.1    Stage 1: Expert-Based Guidelines
The development of these Stage 1 expert-based guidelines has been based on the current state of science
known to experts in the field of blast exposure monitoring and adverse brain health outcomes in military
training and operations. There is a level of opinion present in expert-based guidelines which may be derived
from the individual expert’s knowledge and training. These guidelines provide an expert-based framework
for military personnel and leaders to develop procedures to minimise exposure and associated risk of
adverse brain health outcomes in the short and long term. This RTG HFM-338 report outlines these expert-
based guidelines.

1.3.2    Stage 2: Research-Based Guidelines
The research-based guidelines will enhance effective collection of data and the development of necessary tools
for documentation of exposure levels, diagnostics, and procedures to monitor the cumulative exposure
and associated health status over a longer period. This will result in specific knowledge on the acute
and long-term adverse brain health outcomes linked to exposure history and will presumably lead to more
advanced research-based guidelines. These improved research-based guidelines will result in more effective
procedures for monitoring, clinical assessment, and documentation, which will reduce risk while ultimately
enabling development of evidence-based guidelines in Stage 3.

1.3.3    Stage 3: Evidence-Based Guidelines
Evidence-based guidelines are supported by a mature literature with studies of differing levels of evidence.
Evidence is graded using various methodologies, e.g., the Cochrane Grading of Recommendations Assessment,
Development, and Evaluation (GRADE), and ranked from low to high in quality. High-quality evidence
is weighted more highly than lower quality evidence in formulating guidelines. The application of the guidelines
provided by Stage 1 and 2 in the context of high quality observational and clinical studies will generate data over
time, which will allow long-term brain pathology to be linked to the cumulative exposure of each individual
warfighter. This will lead to the development of evidence-driven guidelines to control the risk of adverse brain
health outcomes due to blast exposure.

Rapid advancement through these three stages of guideline development will be accelerated by collaborative,
multi-nation allied research and sharing of collected data between countries.

            Chapter 2 – BACKGROUND RISK TO BRAIN HEALTH

Whether exposure to Blast Overpressure (BOP) could be related to alterations in brain function (Blast-Induced
Neurotrauma – BINT) has been a matter of discussion since World War I (WWI). Reports of the so-called shell
shock syndrome (described in The Lancet by C.S. Myers (Feb 13 1915, pp. 316-320) and summarized
in Ref. [1]) where soldiers that had been close to detonations but did not have any visible wounds to the head
frequently presented with amnesia, poor concentration, headache, tinnitus, hypersensitivity to noise, dizziness,
and tremor.

Despite uncertainties in underlying pathology and pathophysiological mechanism, the clinical medical problem
with this disorder became significant to such an extent that up to 10% of British battle casualties
were categorized as shell shock-related and 32,000 British war pensions were awarded for shell shock. The term
shell shock fell out of favor and the British Government banned the term at the beginning of the Second World
War; soldiers that were near explosions continued to present a variety of symptoms that were generally
categorized as post-concussion syndrome.

Modern time awareness of BINT stems from the high numbers of reported Mild Traumatic Brain Injury (mTBI)
in recent conflicts starting in Afghanistan and Iraq. The use of Improvised Explosive Devices (IED) in these
conflicts resulted in a growing number of military personnel having been exposed to BOP, which was
considered one explanation to the high number of reported mTBI [2] (Figure 2-1). Improvements in body and
vehicle protection and a resulting decreased mortality but greater morbidity in Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) was
also a plausible explanation for the observed increase in the number of mTBI [3]. Similar to the previous WWI
observations, the early studies of BOP-related TBI in modern conflicts observed an overlap of concussion-like
symptoms and PTSD/depression [4]. Separating the contributions of the different parts of the blast exposure
(primary, secondary, tertiary blast, Table 2-1) from each other and from physical, blunt impact is important to
understand underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and consequences of BINT. For example, MacDonald et
al. showed specific brain imaging findings related to axonal injuries in U.S. military personnel that had been
exposed to blast and showed symptoms of mTBI; however, these individuals were also exposed to other blunt
impact, complicating the possibility to separate the different mechanism of injury [5]. Multiple more recent
studies have attempted to separate out the specific contribution of especially the primary blast to BINT. These
have shown promising findings on regulation of brain injury markers [6], [7], [8], [9], specific brain imaging
findings [10], [11], [12], and cognitive changes [13]. The current scientific clinical knowledge about BINT
suggest symptoms in the hours and days after repetitive BOP exposures, particularly those of higher intensity,
may share features with mTBI, i.e., impairment of memory, particularly working memory, confusion, altered
consciousness, slow thinking, slowed reaction time, headache, etc. Similar to mTBI, initial acute symptoms may
diminish. However, cumulative repetitive exposure over time is associated with increased changes in brain
structure and function, altered brain biomarkers, and an increase in symptoms relating to mental health,
such as depression, anxiety, mood alteration, irritability, etc. Additionally, operators with higher levels
of cumulative exposures seem to show a lower threshold for the manifestation of signs and symptoms following
blast exposure than personnel with lower lifetime levels of exposure.

These intensified clinical and experimental research efforts in combination with the technical development
of wearable blast sensors (measure of BOP exposure) during the last 10 – 15 years, as well as structured
self-report tools for the assessment of cumulative BOP exposures since 2020, have resulted in a growing body of
data on the blast wave and its role in BINT (primary effect). This, in turn, has increased the understanding
of the importance to better understand the affect of BINT on warfighters’ health and performance.

The fact that the primary effect of BOP exposure, without secondary effects (penetrating injury from fragments)
or tertiary effects (e.g., movement of the head) can result in BINT, has resulted in multiple actions in the military
medical field. One example is the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) and the Department of Veterans Affairs
(VA) in 2021 requesting a diagnosis code specifically for blast injury of the brain from the International

Classification of Disease, 10th Revision (ICD-10) Coordination and Maintenance Committee. ICD-10
is the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, which is the global World
Health Organization (WHO) standard for diagnostic health information. It was pointed out in this request
that the level of evidence of a direct primary blast wave effect on the brain is now sufficient to refer
to it as a unique clinical entity which merits a unique diagnosis code, even though details of its fundamental
injury mechanisms are not fully understood. This request led to the decision by the World Health Organization
(WHO) to classify brain injuries from primary blast exposure as a unique clinical entity according
in the International Classification of Disease (ICD) system; in 2022 ICD-10 established the code S068A
for Primary Blast Injury of the Brain [14], [15].

Parallel to this developing knowledge of primary BOP exposure being a source of BINT, our understanding
of the brain effects of repeated exposure from low-level primary BOP, over short (e.g., training exercises)
or long (e.g., deployment, range safety officers) periods of time has evolved. The vast majority of such BOP
exposures for individual military personnel have been in the training environment. Advantageously,
this environment is also much easier to capture properties of the BOP exposure and collect relevant health data
as compared to the deployed/combat environment. This has shifted the focus of the BOP health research field
toward the training environment with repeated low-level BOP exposure and the cumulative effects of those
exposures on medical outcomes. There are now numerous studies utilizing more standardized setups for capture
of BOP exposure and acute health outcomes. For example, breaching exercises with explosives has gathered
important new data on primary BOP exposure health effects [16], [10], [17], [7].

One other major result of the increased understanding and concern about the effects of BOP exposure and its role
in BINT and brain health of service members was the United States Congress legislating Section 734
of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2018. This law required the U.S. Secretary of Defense
to conduct an extensive longitudinal medical study on blast pressure exposure of members of the Armed Forces.
The overarching goal of Section 734 studies was to demonstrate that it is possible to capture blast information
from weapon systems utilizing wearable blast sensors, store that information safely, and improve DoD’s
understanding of the brain health effects of BOP exposure from commonly used weapon systems to better
inform policy for risk mitigation, unit readiness, and health care decisions. This study was part of a larger U.S.
DoD Warfighter Brain Health Initiative (WBHI), a joint strategy and action plan between operational
and medical forces. The focus is to:
    1) Optimize Warfighter brain health, specifically cognitive and physical performance.
    2) Identify known and emerging brain threats in military environments, such as blast.
    3) Detect brain injury immediately when it occurs to combat its effects on warfighters, their families, line
       leaders, commanders, and their communities at large.

3.1 INTRODUCTION
This chapter describes the state-of-the-science of blast exposure threshold values. These values may be used
to mitigate the risk of adverse brain health effects due to Low-Level Blast (LLB) exposure. The values discussed
are intended for short- and/or long-term brain health effects.

At the time of writing, there is no international consensus on the blast exposure threshold values associated
with adverse short- or long-term brain health effects caused by single or cumulative exposures. Multiple
countries use an unprotected eardrum rupture criterion to calculate the Minimum Safe Distance (MSD)
for explosive demolition charges. There is no such calculation for brain health effects since the brain injury
response to blast exposure remains poorly understood. This chapter provides more insight into the threshold
values that may be used based on the current state of science.

Published and soon-to-be published research was gathered. From this research, three approaches for acute
and chronic threshold determinations emerged that were selected. This list is not exhaustive and may evolve
over time:
    1) Peak Overpressure
    2) Cumulative Impulse
    3) Lifetime History of Blast Exposure (Generalized Blast Exposure Value – GBEV).

Additionally, a risk assessment framework, based on current efforts within the US Department of Defense
(DoD), was identified and outlined as a notional structure which may use these types of thresholds in a risk
assessment procedure.

These threshold methods are evaluated in Section 3.2 for their level of evidence, applicability
for short- and long-term brain health effects, and usability. The risk assessment framework is described
in Section 3.3. Section 3.4 summarises the interim exposure guidelines.

RTG HFM-338 found the single exposure 28 kPa (4 psi) peak on-body overpressure, a cumulative impulse
over a single day of 219 kPa*ms (31.7 psi*ms), and a GBEV of 200k – 400k as thresholds for blast exposure
to limit the risk of adverse brain health effects. These recommendations are made in the context of an evolving
understanding of the adverse brain health effects of single and repeated exposures. As such, this guidance
is subject to change based on guidance supported by new and emerging evidence.

This section explores three different approaches for blast exposure thresholds. Each approach includes
a description of the method, the threshold value, the level of evidence, applicability, and usability,
and discussion and conclusion.

3.2.1    Peak Overpressure

Method
Current research provides emerging evidence of adverse health and performance effects (e.g., headache, ringing
in ears, neurocognitive performance degradation, sleep disturbance, dizziness) from acute exposures to LLB.
Until the relationship between BOP and brain health effects are more fully understood, it is recommended
that military units in training manage the risk of exposures exceeding 4 psi (28 kPa) as part of training planning
and execution. The US Interim Recommendation for Blast Overpressure Exposure Safety for Brain Health uses
a blast exposure safety guidance of 4 psi (28 kPa) peak overpressure as a threshold, based on published blast
exposure data and health and performance outcomes [1]. The recommendation does not explicitly state
the method of overpressure measurement that should be used. However, the referenced literature predominantly
used on-body overpressures, often using the highest recorded peak overpressure event of the set of wearable
sensors. Therefore, RTG HFM-338 concluded that a recommendation based on such referenced data
should specify a peak on-body overpressure threshold.

Threshold Value
4 psi (28 kPa) peak on-body overpressure (highest point recorded in a shock waveform as measured or simulated
at a point on the body) for a single exposure.

Level of Evidence
Currently, published studies supporting this level are limited, but several trends can be observed
from the findings. Symptomatology (e.g., headache, concentration problems, taking longer to think, etc.)
were commonly reported, associated with exposures above 4 psi (28 kPa) peak on-body overpressure [2], [3],
[4]. Reaction time alterations, a metric for neurocognitive performance, were reported for exposures above 4 psi
(28 kPa) peak on-body overpressure in three studies; however, two other studies did not find such alterations
for similar exposures [4] – [8]; 4 psi (28 kPa) peak on-body overpressure is lower-bound and based
on a combination of neurocognitive performance and symptom reporting.

In August of 2024, the US Deputy Secretary of Defense published a Memorandum titled, ‘Department
of Defense Requirements for Managing Brain Health Risks from Blast Overpressure’ [1]. This Memorandum
rescinded the Memorandum titled, ‘Interim Guidance for Managing Brain Health Risk from Blast
Overpressure’ [9], published in November of 2022 by the US Assistant Secretary of Defense (Readiness).
It establishes US DoD requirements and direction for the management of health risks to US DoD personnel
from exposures to BOP. This Memorandum stated that, until the relationship between BOP and brain health
effects is more fully understood, US DoD Components will manage the risk of blast exposures exceeding
4 psi (28 kPa) as part of training planning and execution, and will apply risk management procedures
described in US DoD Instruction (DoDI) 6055.01, ‘Safety and Occupational Health (SOH) Program’ [10].
It specifically states that the policy is not meant to preclude or unreasonably restrict commanders from
conducting mission-essential weapons training but instead establish requirements for practical risk
management action to mitigate and track BOP exposures. It also maintains the interim BOP exposure safety
guideline of 4 psi (28 kPa).

Applicability for Short- and/or Long-Term Brain Health Effects
The threshold value is based on data focused on short-term brain health effects. The long-term effects of
repetitive exposure are the subject of ongoing research [11] – [14].

Usability
While not specified in US policy, the preponderance of the evidence that supports the guideline is based
on body–load data from wearable blast sensors, often using the highest recorded event of the set. The peak
overpressure threshold can be used, with multiple assessment methods described in Section 4.4.1.3 Methods
and Technologies from Blast Exposure Monitoring Capability. These include documented overpressure
signatures from weapon systems and explosives, wearable blast sensors, static blast sensors, and advanced
modelling tools. However, these methods are operating at different levels, and each method has its caveats. It is
recommended to use the best method available, defined by the most usable and most representative of peak
overpressure measured on the body. When methods are used that do not capture on-body overpressure, careful
attention should be paid to how the method, and associated overpressures, translate to the on-body overpressure
and the associated threshold. The caveats of the different methods for blast exposure monitoring are described
in more detail in Section 4.4.1.3.

Discussion
Different methods of assessing whether an individual has exceeded 4 psi (28 kPa) can be used. A wearable
blast sensor will measure on-body overpressures at locations of the body, a static pencil probe will measure
incident pressures at a given location (when properly positioned), and simulations or a reference table listing
exposures may provide all types of overpressures (on body, incident, and reflected) but it will
be representative of a single scenario which may or may not be indicative of future exposures. Each
assessment method and type of overpressure requires understanding of what is being measured. In many
cases, use of multiple techniques is warranted to leverage the respective strengths of each. Strengths
and weaknesses of each method are detailed in Section 4.4.1.3.

The scientific evidence for the threshold level is limited but growing and primarily based on acute short-term
brain health effects. The long-term brain health effects from exposures exceeding 4 psi (28 kPa) remain
unknown, but using such a threshold is a start to limit exposures.

Linking a peak overpressure threshold to an impulse value may improve this single exposure guideline. Impulse,
having both components of peak pressure and duration, may be a candidate metric for improving future
thresholds. Emerging data are starting to provide the first threshold values (see Cumulative Impulse
Section 3.2.2).

Investigations should be conducted on military personnel from various nations who are exposed to blast
overpressure to identify other symptoms associated with blast exposures. The peak overpressure exposures
measured on the soldiers and symptoms could be compared to support the defined 4 psi (28 kPa) peak
overpressure on-body threshold that is reported in the literature [14], [8], [3] – [7].

Conclusions
The use of a single exposure guideline at 4 psi (28 kPa) peak on-body overpressure is a useful first step to begin
helping military personnel reduce their overpressure exposures using an easy-to-understand metric. While other
guidelines may be more comprehensive, this guideline stands out for its simplicity and usability (primarily

when wearable blast sensors are used). Adopting this guideline, along with using the tools to identify
those exceeding the guideline, will allow leaders, medics, and troops to immediately begin controlling/limiting
above-threshold exposures in training.

3.2.2    Cumulative Impulse

Method
Cumulative positive impulse may be calculated from blast overpressure histories, as measured on, or estimated for,
an exposed individual. Blast positive impulse is defined as the integral of the positive phase of the overpressure vs.
time waveform. Total positive impulse refers to the value of the blast positive impulse when it is calculated over
the entire waveform duration. Individual blast overpressure histories may be obtained from wearable blast sensors
on the exposed personnel or estimated from other pressure sensors deployed in the blast environment. If multiple
wearable blast sensors are used, blast impulse may be chosen as the sensor yielding the maximum positive impulse
value or as the average positive impulse from all body worn sensors. Blast positive impulse could also be derived
from overpressure history estimates obtained from modelling of the blast environment. Cumulative positive
impulse is typically obtained from the summation of positive impulses over multiple exposure events,
over a defined period (a day, multi-day training event, etc.), for a single individual.

Threshold Value
Recent evidence, reported below, suggest that a 50% risk of acute cognitive effects may occur at a cumulative
positive impulse value of 31.7 psi*ms (219 kPa*ms), calculated as the sum of all total positive impulses measured
for each individual wearable blast sensor over a period of a single day. The wearable blast sensor with the highest
cumulative impulse reading is compared with the threshold. The measured cognitive changes represent an acute
effect, which may or may not translate into long-lasting effects or chronic impairment. This proposed threshold
also does not account for multiple successive days of exposure. It is also not directly applicable to incident pressure
recording in an environment, as it was derived using sensors recording on-body overpressure (i.e., overpressure
applied on the body surface). It was also derived from computing total positive impulse over a given recording
duration, which is specific to the sensor technology used in the underlying research.

Level of Evidence
Peak overpressure alone is an incomplete description of the loading exerted by a blast wave on personnel.
While peak overpressure quantifies the magnitude of the loading, impulse is a better correlate of overpressure
exposure over time, as it indirectly accounts for how long the loading from the blast is applied, how blast
reflections from the surrounding may influence loading, and how the decay from the peak overpressure
to ambient overpressure occurs.

There is indirect evidence that suggests that blast impulse should be considered in the prediction of brain injury
risk from exposure to blast. Connected fields of research have established that the resistance of structures,
including the human body, to external loading is generally a function of loading magnitude and duration
of application. For man-made structures, failure thresholds are typically defined in the pressure–impulse plane
(also referred to as PI curve), where combinations of peak pressure and impulse values are correlated to a given
level of damage [15]. Burn injury models are also defined as a function of temperature and duration of exposure
[16]. In the field of head injury biomechanics, multiple proposed injury criteria (e.g., head injury criteria, head
impact power) are a function of the loading impulse, which indirectly accounts for duration of load application
by considering head velocity (i.e., integration of acceleration over time) in their correlation to injury risk

                                                                        INTERIM EXPOSURE GUIDELINES


(e.g., brain injury criterion, DAMAGE [17]). Blast injury criteria for organs other than the brain, derived from
animal and cadaveric experiments, have been defined as iso risk curves in the pressure–impulse plane. Eardrum
rupture thresholds [18] and lung injury thresholds [19] are defined as a series of possible combinations of peak
pressure and positive phase impulse values.

For loading from blast waves, duration of loading may also have additional implications. During blast exposure,
the development of the transient overpressure field around the body is on the same timescale as the propagation
velocity of the blast wave. For example, for short-duration blast waves, overpressure on one side of the body
may have time to decay completely before the blast wave reaches the other side of the body. Similarly,
a long-duration blast may engulf the body in a zone of overpressure. These result in significantly different
loading scenarios from the perspective of the body.

Despite the aforementioned evidence suggesting that impulse is an important parameter in the description of blast
loads, only a few researchers have attempted to directly relate blast impulse to a risk of neurobehavioural
impairment. In a recent review of the evidence supporting the use of a 4 psi (28 kPa) maximum peak overpressure
threshold, it was reported that only six of the 14 published research articles that quantified blast exposure
concurrently with brain health and performance effects had considered average or cumulative impulse.
Upon further analysis, only four had used impulse within the context of a dose–response correlation analysis [20].

The following is a review of relevant literature to provide a more detailed description of the study:
    •   Lavalle et al. [5] measured changes in the neurocognitive performance of 202 participants involved
        in a breaching course. The course involved 2 – 4 total exposures per individual, with peak individual
        overpressure ranging from 2.57 psi (17.7 kPa) to 9.17 psi (63.2 kPa) and cumulative impulse ranging
        from 15.05 to 47.2 psi*ms (103.8 to 325.4 kPa*ms), measured from a wearable pressure gauge worn
        on the shoulder. The authors attempted to correlate blast characteristics to changes in DANA performance,
        administered before, immediately after (5 min), and a short-time after (25 min – 2h) the last blast
        exposure. Changes in Simple Reaction Time (SRT), Procedural Reaction Time (PRT), and Go-no Go
        assessment were regressed against continuous models and dichotomous models of blast characteristics.
        While continuous and dichotomous (over/under 5 psi (34 kPa)) descriptions of peak overpressure showed
        statistically significant correlation to changes in PRT, cumulative impulse did not show significant
        correlation. However, it is important to note that overpressure measurements were taken from a single
        sensor only, which is claimed to represent incident overpressure due to the orientation of the participant
        relative to the blast. Therefore, true loading characteristics may not have been monitored properly,
        as reflected impulse may not scale linearly with incident impulse estimates.
    •   Carr et al. [8] performed an extensive list of neurobehavioural assessments and neuroimaging on 31
        participants (26 students and five instructors) before, during, and after a six-day explosive breaching
        course. Given the nature of the exposures, which involved blast propagation in a complex environment,
        cumulative impulse was chosen as the best characteristic to quantify exposure. In this study, students
        and instructors were exposed to 40 low-level blasts, with an average total cumulative impulse of 51
        and 43 psi*ms (352 and 296 kPa*ms), respectively. Although self-reported symptoms (22 queried)
        increased in 44% and 88% of the student and instructor participants post exposure, the authors conclude
        that the study did not yield clear evidence of neurological impairments. However, when observing
        the instructor sub-group relative to the student sub-group, clear effects were observed on task performance
        necessitating memory demand. The authors suggest that a long history of blast exposure expected
        from breaching instructors may increase the likelihood of developing acute neurological symptoms
        after blast exposures.

be applied independently to different exposure environments or over an entire dataset that includes multiple
exposure environments. However, it is important to note that the dataset at the origin of the risk map is not
necessarily well distributed across the map space and therefore, the applicability of the risk map at the lower
and upper boundaries is uncertain. The authors of the study mentioned that the original intent was to generate
a generalised risk map by pooling a larger dataset that included multiple exposure environments and profiles.
However, this idea was abandoned due to apparent inconsistencies between the individual datasets. A dataset
unique to grenade exposures, for example, appeared to generate a significantly different risk map to the one devised
from the breaching dataset. This observation suggests that environment/scenario-specific maps may be required
to properly capture the risk of acute cognitive impairment from repeated exposures to blast.

Applicability for Acute and/or Long-Term Brain Health Effects
Evidence that supports the definition of an exposure threshold as a function of cumulative impulse were built
from observations of neurobehavioural effects or other brain health proxies following an acute exposure
to low-level blasts. They are indicative of a potential immediate or acute response but do not necessarily reflect
risk of developing or contributing to chronic brain health effects. Linkages between an acute exposure
to low-level blast exceeding the proposed threshold and any long-term neurocognitive impairment remains
tenuous. The logical approach, given what is currently known with regards to the cumulative impulse metric,
is to use a threshold that minimises the risk of acute neurobehavioural effects, with the perspective of reducing
the risk of development of chronic effects. The risk thresholds described here were derived for exposures within
a training day. The application of this method to effects from chronic exposure is pending work.

Usability
The cumulative impulse requires insight in every blast exposure during a training day. Multiple assessment
methods described in Section 4.4.1.3 Methods and Technologies from Blast Monitoring capability can be used to
derive the blast exposure; however, this specific method was developed using on-body overpressure from
specific wearable blast sensors. When methods are used which do not use these wearable blast sensors, careful
attention should be paid to how the method and associated overpressures translates to the on-body overpressure
and the associated threshold.

Discussion
At this time, the injury risk function developed in the work of Carr et al. [23] represents the most usable
research that accounts for the contribution of cumulative impulse to the level of risk of impairment.
The threshold value of 31.7 psi*ms (219 kPa*ms), associated with a 50% risk level, represents the level
where the statistical confidence is the highest. However, it is important to note that this risk level may be deemed
too high and therefore, different groups may choose to use daily cumulative impulse values that are lower.

                                                                          INTERIM EXPOSURE GUIDELINES


Also, the applicability of the injury risk function from [23] for conditions differing significantly
from breaching/demolition training scenarios should be considered with caution. More specifically,
the application of the risk curve for larger number of exposures to very low peak overpressure, or for very lower
number of exposures to high overpressure, remains tentative.

It is important to identify the limitations to the usability of the risk curve defined in [24]. First, the study used
on-body overpressure measured from wearable blast sensors. These represent on-body overpressure and should
not be confused with incident overpressure measurements/estimates obtained from an overpressure mapping
exercise. The proposed risk curve should only be used in conjunction with on-body overpressure measurements,
or estimates obtained from simulation. All of the caveats outlined in usability for the peak overpressure threshold
apply. Second, the calculation and summation of single blast positive impulse requires a common methodology
to yield consistent results across different data collection and monitoring efforts. This may be difficult to achieve
since the study reported in [23] used sets of three to eight sensors but does not appear to be consistent
throughout. Averaging of positive impulse over three or eight sensors will likely yield a different value,
depending on their position on the body. In general, the application of the proposed threshold requires
the computation of an average total positive impulse over multiple locations on the body, therefore a maximum
number of sensors is recommended to maximise the accuracy of this average value. Other limitations related
to logistics, cost, and data management may drive decisions on the number of sensors being used. While most
published reports have used three sensors distributed on the body, other sensor configurations may be exploited
to compute an average total positive impulse value compatible with the proposed threshold. The duration over
which positive impulse is calculated from an individual overpressure waveform is critical and should
be consistent with the method used in the work supporting the definition of the risk curve. That work used
the total positive impulse, where the collected data was limited to 17 msec (post rising edge) based on the sensor
used. Application of this threshold to other types of blast exposure should consider potential reflections
in the environment and the appropriate waveform duration to include in a positive impulse calculation so only
exposures potentially linked to brain injury are included.

Adequate post-processing of the raw sensor signals is also required to avoid potential errors due to a shift
in sensor baseline or due to low signal-to-noise ratio. The process for cumulative positive impulse calculation
should be automated sufficiently to allow for monitoring over short timeframes, such as day-to-day
in the context of a training event.

It has been demonstrated in other blast-related and injury-related fields of research that the resistance
of structures, including the human body, is generally a function of loading magnitude and duration
of application. Although still limited, there is recent evidence suggesting that blast cumulative impulse
may be a correlate for the risk of acute neurobehavioural/neurocognitive impairment. A cumulative impulse
threshold of 31.7 psi*ms (219 kPa*ms) (50% risk level) over a day of exposure to low-level blast may be used
with caution, and in conjunction with other proposed exposure thresholds, to minimise the risk of negative brain
health and performance effects.

It is important to note that the analysis yielding the proposed threshold was not designed to accommodate
other exposure parameters, such as time duration between exposure events, which are assumed to have relevance
to outcomes of interest. This method was designed to be usable in the near term, using primary quantifications
of blast exposure, and will likely evolve over time as further evidence is gathered in emerging blast exposure
monitoring programs.

Conclusions
The use of a repetitive exposure guideline of 31.7 psi*ms (219 kPa*ms) on-body cumulative impulse
over a single day is a useful first step to begin helping military personnel reduce their risk of reaction time
decrements over a day of training. While factors such as time between exposures, effect of multi-day training,
and linkage to long-term neurocognitive deficits have not yet been evaluated, adopting this guideline
will reduce risks for the warfighter, including potential safety concerns with neurocognitive deficits during
a training exercise.

3.2.3     Lifetime History of Blast Exposure (Blast Exposure Threshold Survey and Generalized
          Blast Exposure Value)

Method
The Blast Exposure Threshold Survey (BETS) is a self-report tool with two components and designed
to be completed in 5 – 10 minutes. The first component is a blast exposure estimator that queries the number
of exposures across five categories of weapon systems. These categories include:
     1) Small arms,
     2) Large arms (including shoulder-fired),
     3) Artillery (or large weapons carried by vehicle),
     4) Small explosives, and
     5) Large explosives.

Respondents are asked to estimate the number and frequency of exposures within each category. The second part
of the BETS is a cursory symptom estimator. A truncated form of the Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory
(NSI) is used, along with questions related to post-traumatic stress (PTSD) symptoms. The BETS generates
a total Blast Exposure Count (BEC) for each of the five categories of weapons systems. The Generalized Blast
Exposure Value (GBEV) is a single index value that may be calculated from the BETS survey. This value
was derived from an algorithm that was formulated from the initial study sample of 984 respondents [25].
The GBEV is calculated by applying weighting factors to each of the categorical BEC values derived
from the BETS through the following formula:
            GBEV = 0.976(1BEC) + 288(2BEC) + 41(3BEC) + 77(4BEC)(4freq) + 75(5BEC)(5freq)

The weighting factors for the GBEV were defined from the original BETS study by determining the individual
contributions of the blast counts within each weapons system category to dose–response thresholds identified
by examining the NSI and PTSD outcome data derived from the second component of the BETS [25]. Weapons
and explosives that produce higher-intensity blast overpressures contribute disproportionately to the overall
GBEV score. 1BEC, relating to the small arms category, is minimally weighted, whereas 4BEC and 5BEC,
relating to small and large explosives, respectively, each have very high weights. The freq weightings
that are applied to 4BEC and 5BEC describe the percentage of exposures that occurred two days in a row
and are expressed as the decimal equivalent of that percentage + 1.

Threshold Value
The initial publication describing the BETS/GBEV identified a GBEV threshold value of 200k for self-report
symptoms related to ringing in the ears and forgetfulness and 400k for self-report symptoms hearing loss


3 - 10                                                                                        STO-TR-HFM-338
                                                                         INTERIM EXPOSURE GUIDELINES


and changes in taste/smell [25]. Since the initial work, additional studies have been performed that have helped
to refine this value using advanced imaging and other highly sensitive measures in specific military populations,
such as those exposed to artillery rounds. Specifically, changes in neuroimaging structure and function are seen
across multiple principal components clusters at 3BEC values of 12,600 and above. Studies are planned
or underway to define comprehensive dose–response thresholds for cumulative blast exposure for the other
weapons system categories within the BETS (1BEC, 2BEC, 4BEC, and 5BEC).

Level of Evidence
The BETS was developed and initially administered through an anonymous online survey of 984 respondents across
the US DoD [25]. Significant changes at the 200 – 400k threshold levels were observed in hearing loss, ringing
in the ears, changes in taste/smell, and forgetfulness in this study. The GBEV was developed from the contributions
of individual weapons systems categories to the thresholds associated with these self-report symptoms.

Applicability for Short- and/or Long-Term Brain Health Effects
The BETS/GBEV is a self-report tool that estimates a career history of blast exposure. Studies conducted to date
have demonstrated association with longer-term symptoms and objective brain health measures [14], [26].
As such, the tool has been used primarily to understand risk associated with long-term brain health effects.
The tool has also been discussed as a potential measure to stratify risk for experiencing acute brain health effects
based on prior blast exposure.

Usability
The BETS is a self-report tool that can be completed in approximately 5 – 10 minutes. As such, it is a low-cost,
readily deployable solution with a high level of usability.

Discussion
The BETS is a useful tool that allows for the efficient collection of information related to prior exposures
to repeated blast over a career. The tool collects information across five different weapons system categories,
from small arms to large explosives. Based on the answers to the provided questions, total number of exposures
for each weapon system is calculated over a career. Additionally, information on overall proportion of exposures
two days in a row or more are also collected to determine a frequency factor for each weapon system.
The GBEV is a single number that represents a lifetime history of blast exposure. That number is calculated
based on the formula described above. Since the initial BETS study, the BETS/GBEV tool has been employed
by a number of research programs that have conducted studies acquiring brain health outcome measures
alongside the BETS/GBEV that extend well beyond the original BETS study. These measures include
neuropsychological assessments, extended symptom inventories, fluid biomarkers, neuroimaging, and functional
assessments. The number of warfighters assessed across these programs are now beyond 16,000 and counting.
In addition to being employed as a key measure for research, the BETS/GBEV is being administered as a part
of the USSOCOM Comprehensive Assessment Symptom History (CASH) screening program [27].

The BETS/GBEV was incorporated into a study exploring neuroinflammation in special operators recruited
from US Special Operations Command (USSOCOM)7. Significant correlations were seen between prior blast
exposure, as reflected by the GBEV and multiple brain health outcome measures, including changes in cortical
thickness and volume, functional connectivity, neuroimaging of neuroinflammation, and brain-derived
extracellular vesicle concentrations of Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) and Neurofilament Light Chain
(NfL) derived from serum. The study employed Principal Components Analysis (PCA)-based approaches

INTERIM EXPOSURE GUIDELINES


to define the critical thresholds methodology that is being used in subsequent studies to define comprehensive
dose–response thresholds for cumulative blast exposure.

BETS/GBEV was incorporated into the Experienced Artillery Study [28], where, using the critical thresholds
approach, preliminary analyses demonstrate positive correlations with multiple neuroimaging measures
of cortical thickness, alterations in white matter, and changes in cerebral blood flow, along with self-report
inventories reflective of symptoms associated with concussion and psychological health. These analyses
have identified changes in neuroimaging structural and function at BEC3 values of 12,600 and above.

With respect to the overall validity of the BETS/GBEV approach as a tool for defining lifetime exposures
and thresholds for cumulative blast exposure, a recent psychometric study conducted by the US Defense Health
Agency’s Traumatic Brain Injury Center of Excellence (TBICoE) assessed the convergent and discriminative
ability of the BETS/GBEV [29]. For this study, the GBEV was calculated from BETS data, along with modified
GBEV indices, which incorporated BEC2-5, BEC3-5, and BEC4-5 data. GBEV was not significantly correlated
with any of the three non-blast-related variables that were incorporated in the study. In contrast, the GBEV
was significantly correlated with all blast-related variables, which included the number of prior combat
deployments, variables associated with low-level blast exposure, combat exposure, number of combat
deployments, and high blast risk Military Occupational Specialty (MOS). A stronger association was seen
between the modified GBEV indices that incorporated higher-intensity weapon systems.

A separate study demonstrated that high lifetime blast exposure, as indicated by the BETS/GBEV,
was associated with worse warfighter brain health following mild traumatic brain injury [30]. Specifically,
103 warfighters and veterans with a medically documented diagnosis of mild TBI were recruited and subdivided
into high vs. low blast exposure based on the BETS/GBEV. In the absence of clinically meaningful PTSD
symptoms, participants with high lifetime blast exposure had worse scores on the majority of neurobehavioural
measures. Additionally, the high lifetime blast exposure sub-group consistently had a greater number of elevated
scores compared with the low lifetime blast exposure group.

Conclusion
The BETS/GBEV is a self-report tool to estimate career history of blast exposure. The tool is efficient, readily
deployable, and demonstrates high convergent and discriminative validity. It has enabled research to identify
cumulative dose–response curves in blast-exposed military personnel. Additionally, it has seen initial use
in operational populations within the US as a screening tool. A GBEV threshold value of 200k – 400k
is proposed to mitigate risk for long-term neurocognitive deficits from cumulative blast exposure.


3.3 RISK ASSESSMENT
The risk assessment framework is a means to assess and document risk. It combines Hazard Severity (HS)
and hazard probability in a Risk Assessment Matrix through which a risk assessment code can be derived.
This type of structure has been widely utilised for occupational health and safety programs. In the US, a framework
similar to this has been utilised to establish an allowable number of rounds for each weapon system/munition
for hearing preservation, following US Technical Guide 351, ‘Health Hazard Assessor’s Guide’ [31].

Current methods to predict and assess the HS and HP associated with exposure to blast are focused primarily
on the lung, other air-containing organs, and the ears as the most vulnerable to injury [31]. However,
the observed response to blast for tissues such as the lung result from exposures several scales of magnitude
greater than is typical for repeated low-level blast exposure measured during Tier 1 weapon use [32]. In the US,

assessment processes assign HS and HP in accordance with US Military Standard (MIL-STD) 882E and require
health hazard exposure thresholds to be clearly defined [33]. The dose–response relationships between blast
exposure (dose) and adverse brain health outcomes (response) are not yet fully understood. The intent
of this section is to outline the framework for risk assessment and how it might be implemented as our
knowledge of dose–response evolves. Implementation of this framework is currently being explored by the US
DoD as an Interim Brain Health Protection Criteria (HPC).

The HS represents the magnitude of the adverse health outcome to a system user that will occur from exposure
to a hazard during normal use of the system and the HP represents the degree of likelihood that exposure
to that hazard will produce an adverse health outcome [34]. Table 3-1 and Table 3-2, adapted

are used. The standard categories and levels may not match with the eventual categories and levels that can

Ultimately, the identified health risk is characterised by a Risk Assessment Code (RAC), which represents

A unique combination of Hazard Severity and Hazard Probability alphanumeric values (e.g., 1A, 2B, 3B)

This method should be interpreted as a framework that can be adapted to incorporate applicable thresholds
as they become available. To apply this framework, cutoffs and conditions to differentiate between the different
HS categories and HP levels must be determined based on appropriate thresholds and dose–response
information. In the context of adverse brain health effects due to blast exposure, there is currently no widely
accepted Occupational Exposure Limit (OEL) for blast exposures to the brain.

Conclusion
When the HS categories and HP levels are defined, the risk assessment framework may help to prevent adverse
brain health outcomes associated with military personnel use of weapon systems. It can be continually updated
as new and relevant information is learned and be expanded to consider additional important metrics such as blast
characteristics, individual exposure history, or individual susceptibilities to injury from blast exposure. It aims
to output actionable risk levels and risk mitigation recommendations in language that is generally familiar
to military personnel. Ultimately, such framework can be used in conjunction with existing assessment capabilities
based on risk of lung injury and hearing loss due to BOP exposure. The ability to assess the risk of adverse brain
health outcomes associated with exposure to heavy weapons and breaching charges will provide commanders
and risk acceptance decision-makers with the information they need to better protect brain health.

3.4 CONCLUSION
RTG HFM-338 has reviewed published and emerging research on blast exposure and associated adverse brain
health effects. Three methods were identified by expert opinion due to their relative maturity in the field
and applicability as a threshold method. These three methods may be used as thresholds to reduce the risk
of adverse brain health effects from blast exposure. A risk assessment framework was introduced to assess risk
linked to severity and will be useful as more data is collected on blast dose–response.

As the current state of science, RTG HFM-338 found the 28 kPa (4 psi) peak on-body overpressure for a single
exposure, a cumulative impulse of 219 kPa*ms (31.7 psi*ms) over a single day, and a cumulative GBEV
of 200k – 400k as thresholds for blast exposure to limit the risk of adverse brain health effects. The peak
on-body overpressure method provides a direct limit for any single blast exposure. The cumulative impulse
provides a daily limit for blast exposures in specific scenarios. The GBEV provides a threshold for multiple blast
exposures over multiple years. Applying the three types of thresholds provides limits for single exposures
as well as multiple exposures. The three methods can be applied separately, and health assessments can be taken
when any of the thresholds are passed.

These identified methods are deemed to provide the most suitable threshold at the time of writing. However,
they are still limited in evidence of mitigating adverse brain health effects. Proven injury criteria do not exist yet
for this type of injury and it is recommended these be developed. Meanwhile, the proposed methods are interim
thresholds, which may be used to start limiting blast exposures and associated brain health risk while
the scientific community works on refinement of these criteria.

4.1 INTRODUCTION
This chapter describes practical guidelines based on the current knowledge and experience of the experts present
in the HFM-338 group. These guidelines are designed to be applicable for all NATO members and allied
countries and will contribute to force health protection, long-term readiness, and the requirements and demands
of the military leadership to address their responsibility as employers. The operational guidelines describe
what measures countries can take to reduce blast exposure and its health effects.

The operational guidelines are structured in four separate capabilities which countries can develop to start
(or to continue) mitigating military occupational brain health risks from repetitive blast exposure. Implementing
these is going to take a multidisciplinary approach from occupational health and safety professionals, engineers,
policy makers, IT-support, and military staff members responsible for safety.

The four capabilities and their connections are pictured in Figure 4-1 and will individually be described
in more detail in Sections 4.3 and 4.4. Starting with Blast Exposure Monitoring (Capability 1) and Capture
of Health & Performance Information & Data (Capability 2) is important to enable future risk assessments
as more guidance emerges on blast exposure safety thresholds. While Blast Exposure Monitoring and Capture
of Health & Performance Information & Data may be performed independently, there are significant advantages
to Analysis of Blast Exposure (Capability 3) being done alongside Blast Exposure Monitoring and Capture
of Health & Performance Information & Data to incrementally refine data elements for monitoring
and associated exposure guidelines, and to refine the health and performance tests and associated thresholds.
Over time, dose–response relationships can be refined for both acute and cumulative exposures. Mitigations
and Health Management (Capability 4) work together to reduce blast exposures and the associated health
effects. Moreover, even without knowing the ‘safe’ exposure levels, a country can introduce Mitigations
and Health Management strategies to reduce blast exposures and the resulting health effects.

4.2 GUIDELINES STRUCTURE
The operational guidelines consist of capabilities, sub-capabilities, and levels. Capabilities are the functional
components of the operational guidelines and are further divided into sub-capabilities. For every sub-capability,
six levels are described, which indicate advancement of guideline implementation. The different structural
components of the operational guidelines are outlined in more detail below:

4.2.1    Capabilities
The capabilities describe the four functional components of the operational guidelines (Figure 4-1).
When all capabilities are implemented, they form a comprehensive program aimed at monitoring and mitigating
the health effects associated with brain health risks from repetitive blast exposure. Individual capabilities
are suitable for functioning independently, but when combined, they complement and refine each other.

4.2.2    Sub-Capabilities
Every capability is divided into multiple sub-capabilities that each describe a different aspect of the capability.
A graphical representation of the different levels for the capability blast exposure monitoring (Section 4.3.1), and

4.2.3    Levels
Levels suggest an approach to developing the sub-capabilities and thereby provide a guide to expand
the operational guidelines. From Level 1, no (or limited) policy on the topic, up to Level 6, maximum effort
toward achievable stretch goals, they specify a logical progression to create comprehensive operational
guidelines. Higher levels include the capabilities described for lower levels. A country may operate at different
levels across capabilities and sub-capabilities as they evolve their country-specific operational guidelines.

4.2.4    How to Use this Chapter
The operational guidelines are described in three principal sections: 4.3 Guidelines, 4.4 Detailing the Operational
Guidelines, and 4.5 Examples. Guidelines provide a concise itemized overview of the topics addressed

narrative description of each item in Guidelines. Lastly, Examples provides several examples that show
how the capabilities, sub-capabilities, and levels function in practice, and how the different capabilities
are connected. From these sections the reader can select a section with the level of detail and practicality
that is most appropriate for their situation.

4.4 DETAILING THE OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES
This section provides more in-depth details of the operational guidelines. It provides a description
of the capabilities and sub-capabilities and describes each level at a greater level of detail.

4.4.1    Blast Exposure Monitoring Capability
The monitoring of blast exposure is essential to the quantification and understanding of the level of risk
associated with a given individual exposure history. The general logical progression of this capability
is an evolution from small-scale subjective monitoring (i.e., self-reported) to broad objective monitoring
(e.g., wearable blast sensors). To progress towards such comprehensive monitoring capability, there are three
key sub-capabilities that a policy should grow along:
    A) Scenarios (‘Where do I monitor’?): Blast exposure monitoring may take place in various
       circumstances and risk environments, which combine to create scenarios of exposure. Scenarios
       of interest may be identified in different ways. A desk analysis based on previously documented
       scenarios involving training with similar types of explosive or weapon systems is a simple method
       of identification requiring minimal resources. Scenarios may be identified from any training activity
       involving a weapon system that is part of an agreed upon list of previously characterised weapon
       systems, or those of high concern. Scenarios of interest may even comprise of any activities (military
       education, training, exercises, and combat) that carry a reasonable risk of blast exposure, such as small
       arms ranges, areas of concussion grenade use, or a tank firing range.




STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                               4-9
OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


     B) Population (‘Who do I monitor’?): Blast exposure monitoring can take place across various
        populations, from only those who had previously self-reported exposure or symptoms
        up to any warfighter at risk of exposure to a blast.
     C) Methods (‘How do I monitor’?): Blast exposure monitoring may employ various methods
        and technology to achieve a given level of exposure quantification. The self-reporting of exposure
        history using a logbook is a simple way to collect information of number and frequency of exposure
        but relies only on rough estimates or notional levels of overpressure. Alternatively, individually worn
        blast overpressure sensors that automatically record and log all blast exposures experienced
        by each warfighter offer the potential for a characterisation of high resolution and accuracy.

4.4.1.1    Scenarios
Level 1: The military organisation has not identified specific training or operational scenarios which it wishes
to monitor in any way. At this level, decision-makers are generally unaware that occupational blast exposure
poses a health risk to its population.

Level 2: The selection of exposure scenarios to monitor is informed by external sources of information on blast
exposures and typically consist of a small subset of exposure scenarios at the higher end of the expected
exposure range. These external sources of information may comprise scientific articles (see Kamimori et al. [1])
and CONQUER weapons summary [2], [3], [4], from the open literature and foreign defence department
policies, like the US Tier 1 weapons list (shoulder mounted, 50 cal. weapons, indirect fire systems,
and breaching charges). At this level, the scenarios to monitor are selected because they are deemed likely
scenarios for which relative or quantitative characterisation data was available, and for which the level
of exposure is deemed significant. The main shortcoming of this type of desk analysis is that blast exposures
in a live environment can be highly variable and affected by small changes in the scenario details. As such,
individual exposures in one country can be significantly more or less than that reported by another study
or country. For example, there is potential risk in assuming that the use of a given weapon system by a defence
force will generate the same individual exposures as the use of the same system by another user group. Finally,
this approach to scenario selection may not consider the likelihood of unplanned events that could cause spikes
in exposure during the use of certain weapon systems or explosive charges.

Level 3: At this level the strategy towards the selection of scenarios to be monitored resembles
that of the previous level, but it is now informed by characterisation data obtained through controlled experiments
that are specific to the defence organisation’s operating procedure. Controlled experiments may consist
in the recreation of real blast exposures while deploying a small quantity of pressure sensors (e.g., pencil probes,
etc.) in select locations to measure the range of blast exposures involved in that scenario. This approach alleviates
the risk involved with assuming that for seemingly similar scenarios, externally obtained exposure characterisation
data is representative of the domestic exposures. Scenarios may be selected based on a specific overpressure
threshold of interest or based on a relative ranking of exposure severity. At this level, scenarios of interest
are typically still limited to a subset of blast exposures representing the higher end of the exposure spectrum.

One of the advantages of a decision based on controlled experiments is that they can be implemented
in the process of weapon system procurement or (ammunition) testing. The main shortcoming of this approach
is that it still does not account for crew variations around the weapon system or explosive charge and, similar
to the previous level, the likelihood of unplanned events causing spikes in exposure are not necessarily
considered. This method also assumes access to blast experts and the capability to conduct full scale
blast experiments.

                                                                               OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


Level 4: Relative to the previous level, a significant increase in the amount of training scenarios selected
for monitoring is expected, as well as the monitoring of certain specific operational scenarios. The identification
of scenarios to be monitored may still be based on controlled experiments, but may now also be informed
by more advanced individual exposure characterisation methods such as personal blast sensors, computer
modelling, etc. Because the number of scenarios to be monitored is increased, it is expected that factors other
than the range of recorded overpressure are considered, such as frequency (i.e., how often is the scenario
occurring) and likelihood of unplanned increased exposure. The selection of scenarios should nevertheless
be focused on what is deemed to be significant blast exposure.

Level 5: At this level the strategies for the selection of relevant operational scenarios may remain
the same as for the previous level, but the scenarios to be monitored now include any training
scenarios involving blast exposure.

Level 6: At this level the scenarios to be monitored now include all training and operational scenarios involving
blast exposure. Note that because of the unpredictable and uncontrolled nature of operational exposures,
the choice of monitoring all operational scenarios does imply access to methods of monitoring that can capture
the occurrence of an exposure along with critical contextual information, at a minimum.

4.4.1.2    Population
Level 1: At the first level, the monitored population is not defined or homogenous in terms of roles and group
affiliation. The monitored population is mostly incidental and a product of other existing health care monitoring
programs without a focus on the effects of blast exposure.

Level 2: At this level the monitored population is still not structured or homogenous in terms of group affiliation
or belonging to a high-risk population. However, it now includes members presenting the common
characteristics of being symptomatic (e.g., headache, cognitive complaints, oculomotor or eye problems,
vestibular or balance difficulties, anxiety and mood changes, and sleep disturbances) or of having requested
individual blast exposure monitoring. There is not a universal monitoring strategy at this level, as the selection
of the population to monitor is more reactive than proactive.

Level 3: There is a coherent and more proactive approach towards the selection of the monitored population.
The intent is to target a population that is still considered small relative to the country’s fighting force
but that represents a high-risk population when combining the aspects of exposure likelihood, levels, quantity,
and frequency. Groups at high-risk may include warfighters, students, and instructors who operate and train with
specific weapon systems and/or who conduct explosive entry. It may be groups exposed frequently to one
system or groups exposed to multiple systems. It may be an infantry company responsible for explosive building
entry and/or use of weapon systems associated with high levels of blast overpressure exposure (e.g., part
of the US Tier 1 weapon systems).

Level 4: At this level the selection of the population to monitor is still based on the highest relative perceived
risk, which is still a combination of exposure likelihood, magnitude, quantity, and frequency. However, the size
of the selected population is expected to grow significantly compared to the previous level. Targeted sub-groups
may now represent multiple infantry and/or artillery companies responsible for explosive building entry and/or
use of weapon systems associated with high levels of blast overpressure exposure. The intent is to target
a medium-sized at-risk population relative to the country’s fighting force.




STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                                4 - 11
OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


Level 5: At this level any personnel at high risk of exposure to blast overpressure is targeted for monitoring.
The definition of high risk is now more absolute as opposed to relative to other exposure schemes (i.e., high vs.
highest). The intent at this level is to grow the monitoring program further to a large-sized at-risk population
relative to the country’s fighting force.

Level 6: At the last level, the population is intended to grow further, and the scope of the monitoring program
now captures all personnel within the country’s fighting force at-risk of being exposed to blast overpressure.

4.4.1.3    Methods
Level 1: There is no dedicated method for collecting exposure data. The collection of exposure data is incidental
and a product of existing occupational hygiene surveys not focused specifically on blast exposure.

Level 2: At this level the collection of exposure data is performed a posteriori using focused surveys
or questionnaires that allows for the estimation of the number and levels of blast overpressure exposures.
Estimates of number of exposures are typically self-reported but may be supplemented by documents
such as training schedules of unstructured logs capturing relevant training events with likely blast exposure.
Levels of exposures are only grossly estimated from calculations based on the amount of propellant/explosive
used or binned into severity categories. In addition to the low resolution of the exposure level estimate,
a limitation of this method is that it does not consider variability in the exposure levels due to changes
in personnel position, posture, equipment, environment, etc. The method assumes an approximate level
of exposure for a given source of blast overpressure, regardless of the finer details of the exposure scenario.

Level 3: The collection of exposure data is done through the maintenance of structured exposure logs,
which capture important contextual details at the group and individual level, and which enables a higher
resolution description of the exposure scenario. Levels of exposure are estimated from documented overpressure
signatures for similar weapon systems or explosive charges in similar scenarios. This method is used to assess
an individual’s exposure or to evaluate the exposure to Similar Exposure Groups by an occupational hygienist.
This method still has the limitation of not considering variability in the exposure levels due to individual
position, posture, and equipment. It also still assumes a specific level of exposure for a given blast overpressure
source but may account for some specificity of the scenario, depending on the data available.

Level 4: The collection of exposure data now relies on higher fidelity measurements, specific to the scenario
of interest, which may be done in real-time using wearable blast sensors or through static characterisation
of the exposure scenario. The wearable blast sensor method has the advantage of capturing real-time data,
which really capture each unique exposure instance, considering the effect of any subtle variations
in environment, body posture, and position for a given scenario. A limitation of such sensors
is that the measurement location on the body may have a great effect on the recorded pressure magnitude
and may not always accurately represent true exposure of the head of the service member, though an algorithmic
approach called FAST-CT has been offered to correctly apply the body-measured values to incident estimation
to convert to head loading [5]. Static characterisation involves recreating the exposure scenario to measure
the source of the blast. These measurements may be recorded by qualified technicians using state-of-the-art
overpressure sensors and/or physical models deployed around the firing position and at any other relevant
positions (e.g., observer, loader, instructor), in accordance with the associated training tactics and procedure
for the weapon or explosive under consideration. The method has the advantage of not being influenced
by on-body placement, and recordings may be further analysed to estimate exposure at the head of the member,
by interpolating the entire overpressure field, or modelling the environment in a suitable computer fluid dynamic
tool. Conversely, it has the limitation of not accounting for variability in environment, person posture,


4 - 12                                                                                            STO-TR-HFM-338
                                                                                 OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


and position, plus significant overhead and expertise required. With both methods, this level is characterised
using higher fidelity measurements to estimate individual exposure. Automation in the data collection and data
processing workflow is not necessarily expected at that level, but the data collection process should be linked
to personal logs.

Level 5: At this level the higher fidelity measurements described at the previous level are used in conjunction
with advanced processing or modelling techniques to achieve a more accurate estimate of individual blast
exposure of the head. Wearable blast sensors may be used in conjunction with algorithms and/or Computational
Fluid Dynamics (CFD) that can process all sensor data from a set jointly to compute parameters that are better
representative of true head loads or head loading conditions [5]. On the other hand, static characterisation data
for given scenarios may be used in conjunction with advanced modelling tools (e.g., CFD codes capable
of handling blast propagation) to estimate the full distribution of pressure on the head in various positions.
To be able to interpret the measurement data correctly it is necessary to record important measurement
conditions. At this level a certain level of automation in the data collection, data process, and exposure estimates
are expected. In the case of the blast sensors, automated data pulling to an analytical platform may differ
between training and combat environments due to digital military communication restrictions in the latter.
Overall, the level is characterised by a greater level of fidelity and specificity of the exposure estimates, along
with a certain level of automation.

Level 6: At the final level, the exposure monitoring method includes one of the methods detailed at the previous
level but is now also coupled with real-time automation of data processing, real-time head exposure estimation,
and data linkage with individual or group logs. Also at this level, the collection of contextual data on the scenario
is now enabled by additional health sensors and/or wearable sensors that can help to interpret the exposure data
(e.g., health, environmental, posture, navigation, body cam, etc.).

4.4.2   Capture of Health and Performance Information and Data
Assessment of blast exposure, health, and performance effects is an important component of a program
to mitigate military occupational brain health risks from repetitive blast exposure. These effects represent
the downstream consequences of blast interactions with the brain otherwise known as the brain health effects.
The Blast Exposure, Health, and Performance Effects capability is organised into three sub-capabilities.
These include: a) Health Domains; b) Assessment Regimen; and c) Data Techniques for Information Capture.
The sub-capabilities below provide additional information on each of these considerations, and are organised
by level, ranging from least (Level 1) to most complex (Level 6). These recommendations represent a synthesis
of what is presently known based on the available evidence:
    A) Health Domains (‘What health data to capture’): The objective of this sub-capability is to best
       describe what areas of health information need to be collected to evaluate for brain health effects from
       blast exposures. Some examples of health data that have been researched in the setting of blast exposure,
       specifically through the US NDAA FY 18 SEC 734 studies, include but are not limited to the following:
       symptom reporting, neurocognitive and neuropsychological, sleep, mental health, sensory,
       such as vestibular (balance), auditory, oculomotor (eye tracking), blood and fluid biomarkers,
       neuroimaging and endocrine function through the collection of hair. Note that these areas of health
       information may be quite sensitive and may reveal effects of exposure that do not represent
       consequences for military personnel performance. Still pending is the definition of criteria
       that differentiate the detection of an exposure effect from the detection of threshold an exposure effect
       that has meaningful bearing on military personnel performance and health.

     B) Assessment Regimen (‘When to capture data’): This part is dedicated to the infrastructures necessary
        to collect and monitor the data and considerations about the analysis plan of the data, starting from one

4.4.2.1     Health Domains
Level 1: At this level no specific health data is assessed. At this level there is not a single test or symptom
specific for blast TBI. Considering the overlap with mild TBI symptoms and tests used for the management
of such health problems it is likely that blast TBI cases are captured within the casualty system developed
for different severities of TBI. Similarities in reported symptoms with conditions such as mild TBI
may also be captured but without necessarily being noticed as blast TBI. Nonetheless, a well-developed TBI
casualty system can be used for retrospective analysis of blast TBI events and resulting brain health effects.

Level 2: This level represents an initial level of self-reporting and recognition of symptoms in relation to repetitive
blast exposure. Military personnel – upon recognising common symptoms after blast exposure, such as headache,
mental fogginess including memory problems, balance issues, sleep and mood challenges – may seek a health
assessment. It should be noted that if acute or chronic symptoms raise to the level of self-report that engages
the health care system, then it is safe to presume that they are serious enough to have interfered with the daily life
activities and job performance of the military member. It is also possible that these symptoms exist at a lower level
but are not sufficient to warrant the warfighter to engage the health care system or the member’s leadership.
In some instances, the warfighter may not report symptomology due to stigma or fear of change of duty.

Acute assessment within hours or days of repetitive blast exposure may have some resemblance to acute
concussion symptoms, whereas assessment of symptoms with cumulative exposures over many months or years
may represent a different array of symptoms more closely associated with post-concussive symptoms,
such as depressed affect, anxiety, mood alterations, irritability, sleep disturbances, and lingering headaches.

Level 3: The hallmark criterion for this level is active engagement to ask about symptoms through a screening
program, in contrast to self-declaration of symptoms, as found in Level 2. This level consists of the development
of an organised surveillance program with standardised tools. There are multiple examples in the literature where
warfighters have reported high incidence and severe symptoms following blast exposures [6], [7], [8].
These symptom complaints may mimic the symptoms reported by those who have sustained a concussion
or mild TBI and may fall within the following main six symptom clusters:
     1) Headache,
     2) Cognitive complaints,
     3) Oculomotor or eye problems,
     4) Vestibular or balance difficulties,

                                                                                  OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


    5) Anxiety and mood changes, and
    6) Sleep disturbances [9].

It is also critical that auditory or hearing-related complaints are assessed due to the dynamics of blast exposure
affecting the ear and auditory apparatus. This is unique to blast exposure in contrast to other brain threats, such as
impact type injuries, that occur during sporting or parachuting activities. There are various symptom questionnaires
(standardised symptom inventory tools) that may be used to assess both the presence of the symptom and the
intensity or frequency by which the symptom is occurring. Two examples of common symptom questionnaires in
North American English and in the public domain are the Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire
(RPQ) or the Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory (NSI). Both questionnaires inquire about all six symptom
clusters noted above.

Tools used should be standardised across NATO countries and should address all six clusters.
There are benefits to using the same questionnaire so that data can be collected across NATO countries
for larger epidemiological efforts.

Level 4: The main advancement for this level is the addition of cognitive and affective assessments.
Neurocognitive and neuropsychological assessments can be helpful in evaluating the effects of low-level blast
exposure on the brain and behaviour. It is important to note that neuropsychological and neurobehavioural
assessments are only part of a comprehensive evaluation for blast exposure. Neurocognitive assessments typically
involve a series of standardised tests that evaluate various cognitive functioning domains, such as attention,
memory, executive function, processing speed, language, and visuomotor/spatial abilities. These domains
of cognitive functioning are cited as components of performance measured by these batteries. These tests can
provide insight into the extent of cognitive deficits that may have resulted from blast exposure. Examples
of computerized tests include the Automated Neuropsychological Assessment Metrics (ANAM), the Defense
Automated Neurobehavioral Assessment (DANA), and the non-computerized symptom inventory Neurobehavioral
Symptom Inventory (NSI). Neuropsychological assessments can also include measures of mood and behaviour,
such as depression, anxiety, irritability, and impulsivity. These measures can provide information about the
emotional and behavioural changes that may have occurred following exposure to blast. It is important to note that
neurocognitive and neuropsychological assessments are only part of a comprehensive evaluation for blast exposure.

Level 5: This level adds sensory system assessments, specifically evaluating auditory (hearing), vestibular
(balance) and proprioception (awareness of the body in space), and oculomotor (vision and eye tracking).
Supervised sensory system assessments have been more strongly correlated with blast exposure magnitudes
and other blast-associated effects than passive symptom reporting.

Auditory function assessment may include assessment of peripheral function (the outer, the middle, and the inner
ear), central function (the cochlear nucleus to the primary auditory cortex), or both. Note that blast exposure-related
hearing ability deficits have cascading consequences on effective interpersonal communications and other
performance, and these effects have been observed even when appropriate personal protective equipment for hearing
has been in place. Recent hearing ability assessments of this type have been made in field settings using a Wireless
Automated Hearing-Test System (WAHTS) [10]) and further such assessments would be found in clinical settings.

Vestibular function and proprioceptive function assessments have been shown to account for performance
variance not accounted for by other measures, suggesting that, when vestibular or proprioceptive deficits
are present, they are less likely to be revealed through other assessments. There are a variety of vestibular
function and proprioceptive function assessments suitable for field administration, including those that require
no equipment as well as some that do require specialised equipment. Examples of assessments in common use

that require no equipment are the Romberg [11] and the Balance Error Scoring System [12]. An example
of a device-based assessment is the Limits of Stability Test [13].

Oculomotor assessments following blast exposure, based on correspondence with Dr. W. Carr 1, have revealed
effects that correspond to magnitude of blast exposure and to elevations of blood-based biomarkers associated
with neurotrauma. Assessments of eye motion include tracking smoothly moving stimuli, converging
and diverging as stimuli move nearer or farther, eye gaze stability following stimuli disappearance, and pupil
response to light flash. An example of an assessment in common use that requires no equipment
is the Vestibular Ocular Motor Screening (VOMS) [14]. There are various examples of device-based
assessment that directly measure and assess oculomotor function with camera technology.

Level 6: This level adds biosample biomarkers [15], [16], neuroimaging [17], [18], [19],
and electrophysiological measures [20]. Biosample biomarkers are inclusive of fluids or tissue derived from
individuals that may be reflective of changes associated with repetitive blast exposure. Most biosample
biomarkers assessed to date have focused on the use of blood samples, with some of the most successful
of these focusing on exosomes or small packets of protein and small molecules derived directly from the brain
and released into the bloodstream. Several studies have examined saliva and hair. Assessed molecules reflect
alterations in a variety of systems, including those involved with inflammation, blood brain barrier integrity,
and a variety of structural proteins. Neuroimaging studies have focused primarily upon Magnetic Resonance
Imaging (MRI) and Positron Emission Tomography (PET). These studies have shown changes in grey matter,
white matter, blood flow, and functional connectivity between brain regions. Additionally, PET studies
have demonstrated increased neuroinflammation in military personnel exposed to repetitive blast. In terms
of electrophysiological        measures,      tools     such      as    Electroencephalography    (EEG)        [20]
and Magnetoencephalography (MEG) [21] exist and may be useful. Of note, several of the techniques
at this level are difficult to assess in isolation at an individual warfighter level and may require a baseline
and/or assessment as a cohort to understand and mitigate effects of repetitive blast exposure. Because
this is an area that is rapidly evolving, it is anticipated that these measurements will become more utilised.

4.4.2.2        Assessment Regimen
Level 1: Blast-related brain health effects are not routinely assessed; however, military health and casualty
trauma systems may be aligned to capture blast TBI-related assessments.

Level 2: Blast-related brain health effects are captured in one point of care time frame. The assessment regimen
is highly dependent upon how the warfighter self-reports symptoms. An example of a clinical tool
that could be used to assess a warfighter after exposure to repetitive blasts that results in self-reported symptoms
is the Military Acute Concussion Evaluation 2 (MACE 2) [22]. The results of the assessment
will be communicated to the warfighter’s unit and would be documented in their Electronic Health Record
(EHR). Assessment of long-term, non-acute symptoms and their association with prior blast exposure
could be accomplished through retrospective survey instruments, such as the Blast Exposure Threshold Survey
(BETS), among others.

Level 3: The assessment regimen is further refined to use one time point of care measures but adds a detailed
history, which will enhance the information obtained. Active surveillance and monitoring of symptoms
and history can occur after an acute training exercise or an acute adversarial event. Providing a unit
with a standardised symptom and history questionnaire to fill out after these blast events can serve as both

Symptom History, or the CASH. The CASH is used to document a detailed history and symptom reporting
to support treatment or mitigation interventions [23].

Level 4: The assessment regimen is further evolved to include prospective, acute longitudinal assessments,
which may be symptom and empirically based, to assess for brain health effects. Baseline assessments may also

cognitive and psychological functioning in individuals who have experienced low-level blast exposure.

assessments that also incorporates sensory system assessments. Pre-exposure baseline assessments can assist
in evaluating changes post-repetitive blast exposure. The subacute assessment timepoints would serve
to evaluate return to baseline or the presence of more long-lasting effects. An annual assessment regimen, maybe
in conjunction with an existing assessment regimen (e.g., hearing conservation program), could serve to track
change commensurate with chronically exposed populations, relative to populations without exposure.

Level 6: Level six incorporates prospective, long-term longitudinal assessments with health monitoring.
This level is distinguished from the prior level as encompassing an entire military career of assessments.
Like other occupational risks, such as radiation exposure, this level recognises that repetitive blast exposure
is a risk throughout a career and should be captured as such. This would include capture of baseline information
at the start of service.

4.4.2.3    Data Techniques for Information Capture
Level 1: At this level no documentation specifically related to brain health effects from blast exposure
is obtained. However, information already in the casualty management and trauma systems are ideally optimised
for information capture of these conditions and could, with modifications, be optimised for capture of health data
related to blast TBI. At this level blast overpressure and its brain health consequences are not treated as a specific
medical entity.

Level 2: As a first line approach for repetitive blast exposure, symptom data assessment and capture is through
an encounter with the military treatment facility. Some specialised units that are considered high risk for blast
exposure or blast injury, such as Special Operations Forces (SOF) or Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD), may
have advanced reporting infrastructure. Retrospective queries of these specialised unit databases and records
may provide additional information.

Level 3: Capture of data at this level resides within the military unit. Depending on the area of specialty,
some units have infrastructure to support symptom-based questionnaires. However, another important aspect
at the unit level is to assure that someone in the unit has access and can evaluate the symptom data as well
as a detailed history profile. If the data is not available then opportunities for intervention, treatment,
and/or mitigation are negated.

Level 4: Data techniques for information capture expands to the organisational level. Infrastructure exists
to obtain, maintain, and store records of blast-related brain health assessments and measures at the organisational
level. The data platform allows for analysis both at the individual warfighter level as well as the organisational
level to evaluate trends and outliers. At this level there is more health-related assessment data which gives more
power for the data analytics to yield actionable information.

Level 5: This level expands to include infrastructure to obtain, maintain, and store records of blast-related brain
health assessments at the national level. Due to the size and magnitude of the brain health assessment data across
a nation, these data merit a dedicated system or subsystem that affords the ability to compare assessment results
over repeated administrations and long durations. Unstructured data in a general medical record system may
preclude routine time-based comparisons and therefore obscure exposure-based effects while they are occurring.
However, national-level data systems should support real-time analysis of blast-related brain health assessment
information.

Level 6: This level is distinguished from the prior levels in that it addresses techniques for capture rather
than the setting of information capture. These techniques may be applied to Levels 2 through 5
in this sub-capability. This level employs the use of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning (AI/ML)
techniques for efficient and accurate information capture. These techniques may handle unstructured up to highly
structured data, with ingestion of that data and translation into a uniform format. These approaches are well-suited
for operational environments, where it is important that any assessment regimen is minimally disruptive and highly
efficient. AI/ML techniques are used in this context for information capture and organisation alone. Use of these
techniques for analysis are detailed in the Analysis of Blast Exposure, Health & Performance Effects section.

4.4.3    Analysis of Blast Exposure, Health and Performance Effects
The exploitation of monitoring data mostly relies on the capability to efficiently assess and analyse the collected
blast exposure and health and performance information. This information may be analysed independently
to provide multiple direct opportunities for mitigation and interventions. For example, individual exposure data
may be analysed relative to group average exposure to identify outliers to intervene on, or levels of specific
biomarkers may be tied to measurable performance decrement to identify threshold values not to be exceeded.
Nevertheless, the combined and integrated analysis of the exposure and health and performance information
presents significant benefits. Not only can it provide supporting evidence to clinical diagnostics, but it can
contribute to further reinforcing the causal link between blast exposure and adverse health and performance
effects. Perhaps even more important, the integrated analysis of exposure and health and performance data
is critical to progress towards the identification and refinement of safe blast exposure limits.

The analysis capability are therefore divided into three sub-capabilities:
     A) Health and Performance Analysis: This sub-capability captures the level of sophistication
        of patient-reported and clinician assessment data. The intent is to develop a health and performance
        assessment suite to enable accurate derivation of subject baseline health and performance
        as well as detect deviation from this baseline.



4 - 18                                                                                             STO-TR-HFM-338
                                                                                    OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


    B) Exposure Analysis: The capture of blast overpressure exposure through increasingly detailed
       measurement and characterisation builds this sub-capability. Refinement of this sub-capability
       progresses towards a better estimation of on-body overpressure loads and translation of these loads
       to tissue stress/strain.
    C) Data Integration: This sub-capability is intended to describe the development of risks associated
       with dose response information. Specifically, the integration of health and performance data along
       with exposure data enable increasingly sophisticated injury risk assessment.

As with the previous capabilities, each sub-capability is articulated over six levels of refinement and complexity,
ranging from a basic interpretation of the collected data to more advanced statistical analyses
and up to AI-enabled analysis and interfacing with advanced modelling approaches. The analysis
sub-capabilities can also grow in sophistication with regards to the methods of handling, managing, and pooling
the collected data for analysis. For example, independent and distributed records of information may limit
opportunities for analysis while automatic data aggregation from individual blast sensors into individual medical
records may enable more options.

All six levels are further detailed below for each of the three sub-capabilities.

4.4.3.1    Health and Performance Analysis
Level 1: At this level no health analyses are performed specific to blast exposures. Only regular health care
assessment measures are considered when conducting health analyses.

Level 2: Clinician assessment of subjective self-declared symptoms related to blast exposure. The first line
assessment of self-reported symptoms to a medical provider that could be medically trained assets embedded
within an operational unit or a clinical encounter with a health professional at a health care facility. Clinical
observations of the members self-reported symptoms would be recorded in the member’s medical record.

Level 3: Clinician assessment of objective data collected through standardised clinical assessment. A second line
clinical intervention with additional clinical assessment tools brought into the assessment through routine
surveillance will enable more detailed analysis. The medical provider could be medically trained assets
embedded within an operational unit or a clinical encounter at a health care facility. Clinical assessment would
be a rudimentary neurological assessment, such as a mini mental status examination, together with other basic
clinical workup measures that could include analysis of serum GFAP, a CT scan, and neuropsychological testing
if warranted. Detailed clinical observations would be incorporated into the member’s medical record.

Level 4: Many health-related tools generate information that is quantitative in nature. Integration of cognitive
assessments or cognition data will enable more detailed analysis. Determination of whether a quantitative value
reflects a ‘normal’ or ‘abnormal’ value may be accomplished by comparing that value to a reference standard.
Such standards are defined by acquiring data from groups of normal individuals to establish a reference range.
If a value acquired from an individual falls within that reference range, it is determined to be ‘normal’.
When compared to baseline levels, significant deviation, or if values fall outside of that reference range, it may
be considered ‘abnormal’.

Level 5: Health assessments may take the form of integrating multiple pieces of data to generate a scale or score
that has diagnostic or prognostic value. A broad range of different types of tools follow this model, including
physiological testing, cognitive tools, and testing of sensory systems. This level is defined by health assessments
that employ this approach. Examples of these include the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) [32] or Glasgow


STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                                4 - 19
OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


Outcome Scale-Extended (GOS-E) [33] as well as the Rotterdam [34] and Marshall Scoring Systems [35].
This level is defined by the implementation of these types of approaches to integrate multiple data elements
into a scale or scoring system to assist with operational decision making.

Level 6: Artificial Intelligence-based tools may serve as powerful approaches for performing adaptive
assessments or revealing latent information within health data. These approaches may be employed to aid
in ingesting large amounts of unstructured data in different forms, identifying key patterns that may assist
with diagnosis, may help with outcome prediction, improve health-related workflows, and assist with decision
support pipelines. Additional data streams incorporated into analysis may include – but are not limited to –
the use of biosamples, biomarkers, neuroimaging, and electrophysiology. This level is defined by the capability
to implement such approaches.

4.4.3.2    Exposure Analysis
Level 1: Monitoring data or an exposure log is generally non-existent and does not allow for any quantitative
analysis.

Level 2: The available exposure data is generally in the form of self-reported exposure history, which may
be complete, such as an estimation of exposure history over an entire career, or partial and focused on recent
training events. This exposure history is generally not logged consistently or in a standardised format,
which makes robust analysis over sub-groups or populations challenging. Analysis is limited by incomplete data
and the low resolution and accuracy of the data available. Exposure may be described in nature or type but not
typically quantified in magnitude other than in relative terms.

Clinicians and leaders may use self-reported exposure history along with generic quantification of the severity
of the exposures, sourced externally for the weapon systems or explosives used, to estimate qualitatively
the severity of the exposure to inform diagnostics and recommendation for treatment. For example, a clinician
may use the self-reported exposure history of a member reporting certain symptoms and identify
that the member has been recently exposed to a high number of low-level blasts of relevant magnitudes,
therefore helping them choose the course of action, such as withdrawing a member from training involving blast
exposure or weapon firing until symptoms subside.

This level of analysis is generally reactive to a warfighter reporting symptoms and not used as a mitigative
measure.

Level 3: The available exposure data is structured and formatted in a way that enables a quantitative estimation
of exposure numbers and levels. Estimation of levels may rely on collected data in real training events
or on generic quantification from external sources but is generally specific to the scenarios monitored (weapon
system or explosive). Metrics to better quantify the exposure history (e.g., lifetime exposure measurement tools,
such as the BETS/GBEV and others) are used or developed to better enable injury risk analysis. Protocols
for injury mitigation may rely on the review of personal logs, which may be done periodically or on demand.
At this level the exposure data is not specific to the individual in the scenario and may come from static
experiments where the blast signature of the weapon/explosive scenario was quantified using pressure sensors
in the environment, or from averaged recordings from wearable blast sensors in a similar environment, but not
necessarily in the same position/posture.

Level 4: Exposure data specific to individuals is now accessible through the deployment of personal blast
sensors collecting all exposure events in the form of pressure–time recordings, or from an experimental/

                                                                                OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


modelling approach estimating individual exposure for someone performing the scenario being monitored in the
same position/posture. At this level the collected or calculated pressure histories are typically analysed directly,
with no further modification/post-processing aside from the standard signal processing done by the sensor unit or
associated software, to remove artifacts, noise, or eliminate false positives. For estimation from an
experimental/modelling approach, logged contextual data from the scenario is used to help estimate individual
exposure data.

The analysis of the exposure data may be supported by software tools which enable efficient data recovery,
scenario identification, logging, visualisation, and extraction of peak quantities, but it typically still involves
significant manual intervention and querying. Quantities or metrics that are analysed mostly relate to total
number of exposures in defined range of magnitudes, frequency of exposures, and single event peak
overpressure or impulse (or averages, or any summation function).

For the case of wearable blast sensors, this level of analysis also provides near real-time opportunities for data
exploitation, such as manual identification of abnormally high exposures (outliers), with the assistance
of supporting staff to recover individual sensor data and monitor it frequently during a training event. It enables
mitigation of exposures from rapid changes that can be applied in training (correct form/position change
in environment, PPE).

In general, this level of analysis adds specificity to the exposure data by relying on more robust measurement
and estimation of individual exposure using specialised sensors, either wearable blast sensors or deployed
in a characterisation arena and coupled to blast simulations. It involves maintenance of logs with individual
exposure data while still requiring significant manual intervention.

Level 5: At this level an even greater specificity of exposure is obtained through more complex analysis
of the measured or estimated individual exposure data. Greater specificity implies added resolution
of the exposure conditions, typically now evaluated at the head of the member. This type of data typically
requires a combined approach, where personal-borne sensor data, collected on the member’s body, is used
in conjunction with modelling or AI-enabled systems to obtain head exposure for each event monitored.

A significant amount of automation is implemented for the recovery, download, and processing of the data
from the sensors worn by everyone. Data is typically recovered without intervention from the user and minimum
intervention from the staff in charge of the exposure monitoring program. For example, this may take the form
of a wireless download, automatically or manually triggered, when being in proximity of a station or a mobile
device. Basic anomaly and/or outlier detection can be done automatically following data recovery and offers
opportunities for real-time exploitation of the data if desired. This may be done using algorithms
that automatically detect when an exposure was abnormally high compared to other similar events,
or to a reference event, and which sends a warning to safety and leadership personnel.

Additionally, the collected data includes full pressure waveforms from a sensor system that can enable the study
of the blast flow field. Typically, data analysis goes beyond simple tracking of peak values and number
of exposures and may include the calculation of more sophisticated waveform features and comparison to injury
risk functions. Data analysis may also use simple classification algorithms to recognise activity types or classify
exposures in various categories or bins.

The linking and logging of the data to personal exposure records and/or medical records are automated. There
is typically a higher degree of data analysis being done on the exposure history, where metrics aiming
at quantifying the cumulative exposure are automatically calculated and monitored. As with single event data,

OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


simple thresholds, anomaly, or outlier detection may be implemented over the entire exposure history,
and warnings may be issued by the data management systems automatically.

In general, this level adds to the previous levels a degree of automation and a higher complexity of analysis
of the single event and historical exposure data, thereby enabling robust dose–response studies. At this level one
would have full exposure and waveforms and ability to look at the entire exposure history. This date would be,
ideally, stored indefinitely.

Level 6: At this level the degree of analysis should be consistent with cutting-edge practices and should enable
significant contribution to the advancement of knowledge in the field. Collected exposure data is now processed
through a data pipeline combining state-of-the-art tools and technology (e.g., neural networks, advanced fast
computing CFD modelling, etc.) and leveraging contextual data from various sources (logs, wearable sensors,
etc.) to obtain a very high degree of specificity on the exposure.

A full description of the level of analysis is not possible as new methods and techniques may continue to emerge,
but conceptually, these analyses should progress towards: generating a higher resolution and higher fidelity
estimation of the individual’s exposure, maintaining a data architecture that enables data science and AI
applications, enabling the full automation of processes, and the integration of blast exposure monitoring
processes to other types of exposure monitoring and health monitoring processes.

To achieve a higher resolution and higher fidelity on individual exposure data, the collected data may undergo
significant manipulation. Sensor data may be used to reconstruct the whole blast of an event and/or obtain
estimates of the local effects on the brain (i.e., brain strains), using validated advanced analytical, empirical,
machine learning or numerical (i.e., CFD, FEA) models. The exploitation of these models may be part
of the automated analysis pipeline.

Robust data architecture is critical at this level to enable deeper data analytics and AI applications. The data
architecture should greatly facilitate the monitoring of an array of potential injury risk metrics. Considering
the amount of collected and processed data generated at this stage, the architecture also serves the automation
of the data analysis pipeline, which is also critical at this stage. While automation is desired throughout,
maintaining the possibility of manual intervention and querying of the data is also important.

The data analysis pipeline is also integrated to other TBI monitoring programs of partner countries to enable
the joint analysis of multiple types of exposure data. The data analysis is also optimised to facilitate
the exploration of correlations between exposure history and health outcomes. At this stage the data analysis
may be exploited in full prediction/mitigation mode, meaning that algorithms can be leveraged to predict
the development of injury according to proposed activities or training schedule, or dictate future training
schedule and exposure limits based on a known exposure history.

exposure and/or measured on-body overpressure.

for Range Safety Staff to mitigate the brain health effects of weapon system training). This data should be used
with caution when applied outside of the scenarios in which the data was collected. The scope of these data
are limited and do not allow for risk function development.

Level 3: Risk assessment and retrospective analysis are the primary intent at this level and may be used in some
mitigation decisions through simple analyses of repetitive exposures. Again, this data should be used
with caution when applied outside of the scenarios in which the data was collected. However, there is lower risk
of extrapolation error due to the improved quantity and quality of the data used in the integrated regression
analyses. These analyses may inform changes to testing environment (see Section 4.4.4.2) and protocol
(see Section 4.4.4.3) with the effect of any changes assessed based on the variability of the pooled data. Health
surveillance data and quantitative exposure data may be automated for basic dashboards. At this level analysis
supports reactive decision-making and is not predictive in the outcome of changes as findings are only correlated
to the current environment.

Level 4: Health and exposure surveillance data used with addition of cognitive function/performance screening
data may be used for preliminary injury risk function development. At this level, the data used enables
multifactor data analysis. In addition, basic computational modelling may be used to start formulating dose
response using simplified metrics (i.e., peak overpressure, cumulative impulse, or lifetime history of blast
exposure [GBEV]). The data includes multivariate risk outcomes (i.e., different functions related to different
health outcomes) which may enable the preliminary assessment of subject specific factors (i.e., sex, morphology,
exposure history, injury history) into more complex analyses.

Level 5: Data at this level is more robust and informed. The intent is to develop preliminary algorithms
for analysis of risk associated with dose response using simplified metrics. Cumulative surveillance data
with additional layers of routine screening data and sensory system data will support development of advanced
injury risk function associated with repetitive exposure and subject specific factors. In addition, advanced
computational modelling enabling waveform analysis using machine learning algorithms may be conducted
to assess exposure and risk of brain health effects.

Level 6: At this level universal TBI monitoring and monitoring of available healthcare resources will be fully
automated. Such automation will enable real-time analysis of risk associated with dose response using the full
extent of the data with repetitive exposure and subject specific factors. In addition, the integration of available
healthcare resources will facilitate the rapid deployment of healthcare resources to detect and/or predict risk
of injury. A transition from the advanced injury risk function based purely on cumulative exposure will occur
at this level such that an exposure profile injury risk function may be derived.

4.4.4    Mitigation and Health Management Strategies
The following sub-chapters contain recommendations for strategies to mitigate blast exposure effects in training
and operational scenarios, with health management based on analysis of the blast exposure and resulting health
and performance effects. The contents are organised by capability level of recognising and counteracting
the potential occupational risks, defined in Section 4.2.




STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                                4 - 23
OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


The operational guidelines for mitigations are structured along four sub-capabilities:
     A) Education Mitigations: The education leg of blast exposure safety is focused on influencing
        the behaviour of personnel (across multiple levels) through the transfer of knowledge on injury
        and exposure to blast overpressure. Personnel are trained on the risk factors associated with the blast
        environment so that they may be able to adapt to mitigate their exposure severity and, if possible,
        frequency, especially with training. Personnel are taught about the cumulative nature,
        short- and long-term health risks, and prognosis of blast exposure and are trained to monitor
        and recognise any adverse health effects so they can seek medical attention in a timely manner.
     B) Engineering Mitigations: The engineering aspect of blast exposure safety is focused on providing
        the methods, equipment, and environment necessary for quantifying and reducing the severity of exposure
        risk, and, where possible, mitigating the injury and improving the prognosis of the exposed personnel.
        Exposure risk is mitigated by changing the training procedures to reduce the threat, or by investing in new
        protective equipment technologies and design concepts evaluated against the quantified baseline. Analysis
        tools, validated by extensive testing and appropriate physical and biological models, facilitate exposure
        monitoring and health effects and provide better methods for assessing exposure risk and proposed
        mitigation and education strategies. Equipment testing programs with advanced manikins
        and computational models will evaluate the efficacy of the protective equipment.
     C) Enforcement Mitigations: The enforcement aspect of blast exposure safety is focused on establishing
        and monitoring organisational rules, protocols, procedures, and guidelines designed for mitigating injury
        and reducing exposure from blast overpressure exposure. Safety officers will provide oversight to ensure
        personnel are using the appropriate equipment and protocols for the applicable blast exposure
        environment, collation of exposure data, and reporting thereof. Protective equipment will be regulated
        and certified to ensure it meets the established technical specifications. Medical officers
        will be responsible to ensure that personnel comply with return-to-duty guidelines and medical readiness
        following exposure and/or injury.
     D) Health Management: The Health Management component of blast exposure safety is focused
        on establishing and developing organisational care systems designed for preventing, detecting,
        and managing injury and reducing subsequent health and biological effects of blast overpressure
        exposure. Personnel and medical force-specific care systems need to improve the health and resilience
        of the force for blast exposure and mitigate deleterious effects where possible. While member countries
        may possess certain capabilities related to the care of military personnel with concussion/mild TBI,
        the framework described below relates specifically to brain health issues related to blast exposure.

All sub-capabilities have an evolving scope of effort, with the comprehensiveness of the guidelines expanding
with each increasing capability level. While blast exposure can lead to a multitude of physical and psychological
injuries, the efforts defined herein are limited to mitigating effects of blast exposure on the brain, with risk
defined as the risk for acute and cumulative, long-term brain injury. The goal is preserving warfighter brain
health and operational readiness.

4.4.4.1     Education Mitigations
Level 1: At this level there is not a concentrated effort to educate operators and support personnel on the negative
health effects of blast exposure and/or methods to identify risk or mitigation strategies. The training planning
should cover known methods to estimate safe exposure limits, including look up tables and Minimum Safety
Distance (MSD) calculations for breaching and EOD activities. Operators should be trained on existing protective
equipment, procedures, and general safety regulations during activities where blast exposure may occur.

Level 2: Countries operating at this level are providing current state-of-science information to educate their general
population of interest regarding matters related to blast effects. The information is disseminated broadly across
the organisation via pamphlets/brochures, seminars, white papers, etc., at a level of detail appropriate for a broad
audience. The information should raise awareness that repetitive overpressure exposures are cumulative in nature
and have the potential to negatively affect brain health and operational readiness in the future. Further,
the information should stress the importance of following all established guidelines and use of personal protective
equipment and weapon-installed protective gear. Personnel should understand the symptoms associated with mild
brain injury and the process to follow for health assessment as well as symptoms to monitor.

The basics of overpressure interaction with the environment should be integrated with the training to raise
awareness of the effect of reflecting surfaces on potential exposures. Operators will be trained in the As Low As
Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) principle for blast exposure. Actions to reduce risk of injury from blast
exposure include:
    1) Minimising the number of personnel in the vicinity of blast overpressure;
    2) Increasing standoff distances from weapons and charges;
    3) Minimising the duration of live-fire events;
    4) Establishing a maximum allowable number of rounds that may be fired during each event
       or time period;
    5) Ensuring application and appropriate use of personal protective gear and equipment; and
    6) Training and education on blast overpressure hazards and risk management actions [36], [37].

Level 3: At this level countries are actively training their support personnel (e.g., range safety officers
and instructors) on the current state-of-science of blast exposure risk, injury criteria, and mitigation strategies.
The support personnel are trained to understand the basic physics of blast propagation within the blast
environment in a limited capacity to determine if operators are at an elevated risk if positioned at certain
locations within that environment. Specifics of this education for each training environment should include
knowledge on blast propagation dynamics, the potential effect of operator positioning relative to weapon system,
charge, and blast overpressure reflections, and structures in the environment. The training for mitigation
strategies should address actions that can be taken for the specific unit to ensure the training exercise mitigates
operator exposure (e.g., moving the operator(s) to a location with a lower risk of injury).

For weapon systems limits should be established on the allowable number of rounds which can be fired
in a single session, a day, and on consecutive days. Training should emphasise the importance of following
these guidelines, and detail how exposure counts are to be made based on distance from the weapon system,
positioning, and environmental factors. Round counts should not only apply to the individual firing the weapon,
but to all of those that are in the vicinity of the weapon, including the support personnel.

Level 4: At this level countries are enhancing the training of their support personnel (e.g., range safety officers
and instructors) to include knowledge related to an individual’s exposure history. Support personnel are trained
on the risks associated with cumulative exposure and have the knowledge to take action to modify training
protocols for individuals deemed at risk due to their blast exposure history, informed by the most recent injury
risk functions (see Section 5.4.3.3 for discussion). Personnel should have a reasonably high confidence
in their estimated/predicted exposure risk based on experiments carried out prior to having humans in the task.
The risk to personnel should be minimised and the setup of the task should be proven to mitigate blast exposure
risk yet still allow the operator to perform duties without hindrances. Personnel should be aware of exposure


STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                                  4 - 25
OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


risks created by straying from the approved setup so they can make an informed decision. A database
of proposed modifications and the modelled and tested results should be built and maintained for sharing
and development of more advanced analysis tools at higher capability levels.

Level 5: At this level support personnel are trained to analyse blast monitoring data in real-time and make
assessments on an individual’s risk of injury during training based on their exposure history and known injury
risk functions (see Section 5.4.3.3 for discussion). The support personnel have training to make immediate
and continuous adjustments to the training procedures to mitigate the individual’s exposure
if they are at an elevated risk. Personnel understand the short and long-term, as well as cumulative effects,
of blast overpressure injuries and understand the importance of continual monitoring before and after
training/missions to secure their long-term health. Personnel understand how the data is used to ensure most
effective use of their specific strengths and weaknesses and what they individually need to look out
for and minimise to lower injury risk.

Level 6: Countries operating at this level are educating individual operators on the blast exposure guidelines
and training them to be able to assess their exposure risk in real-time based on their history. The training
would include several actions that the individual can take to mitigate their exposure risk (e.g., change position
during training). Furthermore, the individual is trained to recognise when health intervention is required
(e.g., awareness of relevant symptoms, high-risk exposure data). Personnel can review and understand system
risk analysis output as applicable to him/her with a high level of confidence in the data presented. Understand
and support continual monitoring both in terms of engineering and medical data and the long-term benefits.

4.4.4.2    Engineering Mitigations
Level 1: At this level countries use established standard safety procedures. There are no additional engineering
controls in place to reduce blast exposure risk other than procedures to calculate Minimum Safety Distances
(MSD) for events that produce blast exposure, such as training environments, weapon systems, and scenarios
that produce blast exposure. The MSD calculations will correlate the size of the relevant blast source and inform
personnel of locations with safer levels of blast exposure. The MSD will not be individualised but will apply
to the standard operator, and no specific mapping of the environment will be involved; however, countries may
utilise MSD calculations that are specific to a known environment (i.e., free-field vs. indoor). For frequent and
repeatable/known scenarios these MSD calculations can be placed in a tabular format for easy and efficient use.

Level 2: Countries operating at this level will have defined guidelines that improve training range design
specifically to reduce blast overpressure exposure. Considerations should include optimised position planning
for the operators based on MSD calculations, location and material of reflecting surfaces, facilities to optimise
or enable safe cover, long-range warning signal transmission, optimised separation between adjacent teams
on the training range, and defined locations of range safety personnel and spectators.

Level 3: For typical training scenarios where there is a high risk of significant blast overpressure exposure,
the environment (surrounding area that observes increased overpressure) should be investigated to develop
an understanding of the incident overpressure profile field at locations where operators are likely
to be positioned. This investigation relies on overpressure mapping data or static characterisation data, obtained
from numerical models or mapping experiments like those described in the exposure monitoring section (Section
4.4.1.3 Level 3 and 4). The same type of dataset may be used for exposure tracking and for informing mitigation
strategies. Experimental mapping of the physical environment can also be used to inform, improve, and validate
numerical models of the environment, or be compared against a numerical mapping simulation. Models can be
used to explore the effect of changes in the environment such as weapon system, charge size, and operator

                                                                             OPERATIONAL GUIDELINES


positioning. Validated models can be used reliably and complement mapping exercises conducted with pressure
sensors and can provide additional insight into the propagation of the blast wave and resultant blast exposure.
Numerical simulations also offer an increased number of monitoring locations as compared to the deployment
of physical pressure sensors. Ultimately, the measurements and/or models provide an estimate of the pressure
profile expected at the head for a given scenario, and this estimation is the metric to be minimised
through mitigation strategies. During a material acquisition, the findings from mapping and evaluation exercises
can be employed as a metric with a positive emphasis placed on material which produces low blast overpressure.
Finally, the integrated exposure and health data (Section 4.4.3.3) may inform changes to the testing environment
for specific site configurations analysed as case studies.

Level 4: This level includes reflected overpressure on the operators, complex reflection scenarios
and the inclusion of physical bodies in the overpressure profile field. At this level the countries will utilise
physical human surrogates and instrumentation and/or numerical models to quantify, model, verify, and assess
risk and potential exposures. Numerical models will be refined based on correlation between numerical
and empirical results. While subtleties in the environment and fine positioning can affect actual operator
exposure, the models provide insight into factors influencing exposure and can guide operator placement
to reduce exposures to the maximum extent possible. At this level the surrogates and models will be well
characterised and fit for the purpose, and the analysts will have a thorough understanding of the intricacies
of the model and surrogate. For example, the inclusion of physical bodies causes obstructions to the blast wave
and must be included (numerically or physically) to obtain accurate blast behaviour; free-field simulations
or mapping exercises must be enhanced to determine the true exposure. When estimating on-body pressure
exposure, the placement of the gauge and its behaviour in relation to PPE must be well understood and accounted
for (i.e., testing over or under PPE may provide drastically different responses and analysts must be aware
of the physics and adapt testing procedures). Standard PPE without blast specific mitigation will be used.

Computer modelling is an efficient method that should be used to standardise training modifications and plan
new scenarios. Quantitative exposure assessment and data collection should be conducted using manikins
with pressure sensors in explosive environments to verify the computer model results. Prior to implementing
new tactics or training exercises, employ experimental setups using manikins and/or perform modelling to assess
the reduced exposure risk.

Level 5: At this level countries are engaged in physical testing of personal protective equipment to evaluate
their efficacy for mitigating blast exposure risk. The evaluation program can be used to develop, test,
and optimise new PPE designed to further reduce exposure to the operators, with appropriate exposure levels
informed by the most recent injury risk functions (see Section 4.4.3.3). Performance of the physical testing
of protective equipment should occur in the blast environment using instrumented dummies, and tests should
utilise wearable blast sensor data collected during training for relative comparisons, when available. Equipment
configurations can be ranked by performance based on the best available information regarding risk variables
and injury criteria. Countries should also consider supporting physical testing with computational modelling
to evaluate variants of protective equipment or different exposure scenarios.

This level will also rely on the use of computational models to design advanced test environments (e.g., new
ranges) that reduce exposure risk, with appropriate exposure levels informed by the most recent injury risk
functions (see Section 4.4.3.3).

Level 6: At this level a single comprehensive tool provides an online, real-time environment to analyse
the required training and operational inputs and make recommendations for maximum loads and risks.
It combines comprehensive system quantification and mapping data with verified and validated system

and personnel models, injury criteria, and assessment tools. These are continuously updated with personnel
operational and training engineering data and associated health data. This continuously improved system enables
the minimisation of cumulative injury potential based on individual risk profiles. At this level, countries
are engaged in a continuous evaluation program to optimise training ranges, scenarios, PPE, weapon systems,
personnel sensors, and models to gain better insight into exposures and to reduce blast exposure As Low
As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA). Exposure and engineering controls are individualised but executed
homogeneously organisation-wide for all relevant military personnel.

4.4.4.3    Enforcement Mitigations
Level 1: Countries operating at this level will follow their existing guidelines for military personnel use
of personal protective equipment such as helmets and body armour to help mitigate blast exposures that can lead
to brain injury. Guidelines should be followed during both training and operations for activities with known
typical overpressure profiles, including breaching, Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD), stun grenades, mortars,
artillery, shoulder fired rockets, and large-calibre rifles (e.g., 50-calibre sniper rifle). Range safety personnel
continually update safety instructions, look up tables, and MSD calculations for recommended standoff distances
for specific explosives, weapon systems, and environments (e.g., breaching, EOD, flash bang, pyrotechnics,
etc.), and following these guidelines.

Level 2: At this level countries are ensuring that their training range designs conform to the latest safety
guidelines based on some knowledge of blast physics. The guidelines will include a blast exposure safety plan
and assessment for training orders associated with the highest risk activities, as defined within each country.
Countries will also enact procedures to continuously evaluate the design of training ranges to identify
opportunities for improvements that reduce blast exposure risk (e.g., reflecting surfaces, defined positions
for personnel and observers).

Level 3: Based on the latest safety information and guidance, countries shall place limits on the number
of allowable exposures for each weapon system, operator position, and training environment, and form as part
of the approved training plan/orders. Health assessments and recent history of blast exposure for everyone shall
be considered when enforcing the exposure count, with SMEs providing guidance on recommended reduction
in allowable exposure counts based on this information. Retrospective risk analysis from integration of exposure
and health response data (Section 4.4.3.3) may inform changes to the training protocols for specific scenarios.

Level 4: Protocols should be developed by each country to define site visits and inspections for training ranges.
These protocols should be updated as new insights emerge from assessment and analysis of blast exposure health
and performance effects to ensure training ranges are adhering to the latest brain health safety criteria. Countries
are not permitting deviations from established training protocols unless a risk assessment using models has been
performed and the blast environment is sufficiently characterised, including an assessment of the realistic exposures
to individuals. Evaluation of potential updates to the training standards are based on unit level modifications.

Level 5: Support personnel (range safety personnel, trainers) should review training plans using experimental
or computational data and assessment tools and advise on training modifications based on the exposure data
collected, including individual dosage. The training plan, including a specific blast overpressure risk assessment,
should be updated and approved by the relevant/appointed personnel assigned to evaluate training risk. Countries
should standardise training procedures at the unit level. Any modifications to these standards should be informed
by assessments of the blast environment, its modification, and any changes in exposure risk based on the best
available injury criteria.

Level 6: Standardise updates to training and operational standards at the broader organisational level.
Specifically, training and/or operational planning shall be based on expected cumulative blast exposures,

will be utilised for everyone prior to mission approval.

4.4.4.4    Health Management
Level 1: Countries operating at this level do not have specific healthcare management capabilities to support
military personnel with adverse brain health effects resulting from blast exposure.

Level 2: At this level primary care is available to military personnel to address the health effects from blast
exposure. These services are delivered by health care providers who are generalists with no specific training
in the management of military personnel with a history of blast exposure. Specific care management
would be anticipated to be largely symptom-based. No specialty services are available to military personnel
for the specific management of adverse brain health outcomes resulting from exposure to blast.

Level 3: At this level primary care services are available to military personnel to address the health effects
from blast exposure. These services are delivered by general healthcare providers with specific training
and expertise in the management of military personnel with a history of blast exposure. Health care practitioners
would base their care upon the latest guidelines for the management of military personnel with symptoms
resulting from prior exposure to blast. Such guidelines may include symptom-based treatment,
as well as rehabilitative strategies or other interventions, to minimise adverse brain health symptoms resulting
from blast, as well as to optimize functional recovery and/or provide treatment for injury resulting from prior
blast exposure. No specialty services are available to military personnel for the specific management
of blast-related brain health issues.

Level 4: At this level specialty referral services are available to military personnel with a history of prior blast
exposure. These special services may include practitioners with training in neurology, physical,
and rehabilitative medicine, mental health, or other practitioners with other relevant skill sets. Specialty
providers would provide focused support to aid with symptom control, rehabilitative strategies, and/or treatment
for injury resulting from cumulative blast exposures.

Level 5: Countries at this level have established multidisciplinary care centres for the management of military
personnel with a history of prior blast exposure. Such centres are staffed by health care practitioners
from multiple specialties that are relevant to the care of military personnel with adverse brain health outcomes
from prior blast exposure. At such centres, there is a team-based approach with effective communication
amongst health care practitioners and the development of a multi-specialty care plan that is specific
for the individual service member. Care teams base decisions on the latest guidelines for the management
of military personnel with this condition. Additionally, multiple care management approaches are available,
inclusive of pharmacological interventions as well as physical therapy, occupational therapy, and mental health
support. At this level these centres are available to a select number of military personnel.

Level 6: At this level the multidisciplinary care model outlined in Level 5 is available to all military personnel
with need across the enterprise. Centres are positioned geographically across a member country within specific
areas to improve access and ensure that military personnel with a need are able to receive support.

4.5 EXAMPLES
Example 1: A country recognises that their dismounted 81 mm mortar causes significant blast exposure,
especially when fired rapidly. Given the risk, leadership decides to monitor whenever training calls for rapid fire
(Blast Monitoring: Scenario 4) but only focus on veteran 81 mm mortar crews (Blast Monitoring:
Population 3). They plan to run a simulation of a common training site and issue wearable blast sensors
to monitor the crews (Blast Monitoring: Method 4). Medics begin screening for early symptoms of blast
exposure (Health Management 3). Data from wearable blast sensors and screenings are stored
by the Regimental Surgeon (Data Techniques 4) for later comparison to symptoms (Integration of Data 3).
Prior to training crews are taught about the risks of high exposures due to improper positioning (Mitigation:
Education 2) and provided lower round count limits (Mitigation: Enforcement 3).

Example 2: The same country also funds its Defence Research Institute to equip manikins with laboratory-grade
blast sensors around an 81 mm mortar in two of the commonly used dug-in firing positions to estimate exposures
(Mitigation: Engineering 4). This team is also given access to the data coming in from the crews and performs
an analysis comparing individual crewmembers’ actual exposures against their recovery time (Exposure Data
Analysis 5). Meanwhile, the Regimental Surgeon’s Office performs assessments on crewmembers flagged
during screenings from the medics (Health Analysis 3) and begins a program to check-in with crews weekly,
asking about symptoms commonly associated with blast exposure, like headaches (Health Domain 3).
They prescribe rest for one day as needed (Assessment Regimen 2).

5.1 INTRODUCTION
The HFM-338 expert panel identified critical areas to further the development of knowledge for blast
overpressure monitoring exposure to mitigate risks to brain health. This chapter outlines the Research
and Development Guidelines aimed at addressing specific gaps in the current practices of monitoring
and analyzing blast exposure data. These guidelines provide a multidisciplinary approach with complimentary
input from the biomechanical, biomedical, and military occupational exposure and implementation domains.
The target audience for these guidelines includes those responsible for implementing blast exposure
monitoring capabilities, those who ensure the accurate and timely transfer of exposure data to medical records,
as well as practitioners responsible for managing the health effects related to blast overpressure exposure.
These guidelines include an overview of the Research Focus Areas, a description of the identified gaps,
and a detailed discussion of the research approach and goals for addressing each gap. The guidelines discuss
how this research can advance the field of blast overpressure exposure monitoring and identify
possible progression factors.


5.2 GUIDELINES STRUCTURE
The Research and Development Guidelines consist of Research Focus Areas, which are further divided
into specific research gaps relevant to each Research Focus Area. Research Focus Areas were developed
by the expert panel who represented experience in multiple areas of research. The following Research
Focus Areas are identified:
    1) Blast Exposure Monitoring.
    2) Load Mechanisms.
    3) Injury Mechanisms.
    4) Injury Risk Function and Thresholds.
    5) Individual Specificity.
    6) Detection of the Brain Health Correlates of Blast Effects.
    7) Mitigation and Intervention Strategies.
    8) Data Elements, Processing, and Analysis.

The intent of RTG HFM-338 was to identify emerging research objectives achievable within the next one to ten
years. These guidelines are divided into two sections: Research and Development Guidelines Overview
and Detailing the Research and Development Guidelines. The different structural components are outlined
in detail below:
    •   Research and Development Guidelines Overview: Each Research Focus Area is presented
        with a brief description of relevant research gaps and details of the research goals associated
        with each research gap.
    •   Detailing the Research and Development Guidelines: The research gaps are described in detail
        with justification of their importance to further the field of blast overpressure exposure monitoring.
        Details include:

5.3.4    Injury Risk Function and Thresholds
The mitigation of adverse health effects hinges on a detailed understanding of the relationship between blast
exposure dosage and human physiological response. An injury risk function is a relationship that correlates
a physical quantity describing the exposure dose, also called an injury criteria, to a risk of occurrence
of a specific injury or negative health outcome. It is a type of dose–response relationship with a focus
on a specific outcome. Injury thresholds may be derived from an injury risk function by identifying
the minimum value of the injury criteria at which an injury or outcome is observed, or by selecting a level
of acceptable risk and identifying the corresponding criteria value. Injury thresholds may represent values
of the injury criteria for which acute or chronic health effects will occur.

There have been several studies attempting to establish correlation between such physical descriptors of the dose
and an adverse acute health effect captured from specific neurocognitive assessment tools, physiological
response tests, or biomarker levels. However, most of these studies still suffer from either a poor or low
characterization of the dosage or from a lack of specificity in the measured response.

The lack of common data elements between studies and the multifaceted nature of the response, where different
adverse effects may have different thresholds, renders difficult the definition of an injury risk function validated
over multiple exposure scenarios. Also, this relationship is likely multivariate, as anatomical variability,
individual sensitivities and pre-existing conditions may influence the risk associated with a given exposure
profile. The gaps and research goals for this Research Focus Area are listed in Table 5-4.

5.4 DETAILING THE RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT GUIDELINES
This section provides more in-depth details of the Research and Development Guidelines. For each research gap
in the eight Research Focus Areas, the research gap, motivation, general approach, and outcome are described.

5.4.1    Research Focus Area: Blast Exposure Monitoring
The gaps in current understanding and methodologies related to blast monitoring and exposure management
are multifaceted. Firstly, there is a notable absence of clear criteria regarding what contextual information
is needed to improve blast overpressure monitoring. This includes factors like body position relative to the blast
wave, environmental conditions, and individual physical characteristics, which may be important for accurately
assessing health risks associated with blast exposure. Secondly, there is a critical need for automated systems
capable of capturing such contextual data alongside basic blast wave characteristics, enabling scalable analysis
across diverse populations. Thirdly, current wearable blast sensors, while effective in recording local
overpressure, may not accurately reflect head loads due to localized effects influenced by body posture.
Addressing this gap requires advanced processing techniques to align sensor data with actual exposure scenarios.
Moreover, there is a gap in understanding the necessary accuracy levels for blast monitoring to effectively
quantify exposure and its correlation with health outcomes. Finally, there is a pressing need to develop biological


STO-TR-HFM-338                                                                                                   5-7
RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT GUIDELINES


dosimetry methods that can reliably assess the physiological effects of blast exposure, complementing direct
measurements and facilitating longitudinal studies to establish exposure–response relationships. Establishing
robust methods for recording individual blast exposure histories using wearable or static blast sensors
is also crucial for mitigating adverse health effects and informing operational and health decision-making within
military contexts.

5.4.1.1     Gap: Lack of Understanding of What Contextual Information is Needed from Blast Monitoring
There is an absence of objective data informing what contextual information is required to best monitor blast
overpressure exposure. Potentially relevant contextual information may include body position relative
to the blast wave, proximity to reflective surfaces, exposure environment (open air, enclosed, confined spaces,
etc.), and body morphology. Additional contextual information will better inform how explosive blast
overpressure affects the body and may be used to more accurately relate blast overpressure exposure to health
risk when monitoring this exposure.

Objective contextual data may be overlayed onto dose response analyses to assess the relationship to health risk.
A multivariate approach may be required as the addition of contextual information may lead to complex
relationships. This will provide leadership, health professionals, and researchers with a better understanding
of the factors that contribute to increased health risk associated with blast overpressure exposure. At the unit
level, troops will improve their ability to operate safely and remain under blast exposure guidelines
while maintaining, or improving, the efficacy of their training and operations. Such information will be critical
to an effective monitoring program.

5.4.1.2     Gap: Need for Automated Collection of Contextual Information
Current blast exposure monitoring sensors typically capture relatively basic characteristics of the blast overpressure
waveform. Determining what additional contextual data can be captured with wearable, environmental, and static
sensors (e.g., could be environmental sensors, cameras, etc.) through automated methods is required. Automated
collection of this contextual information is critical to appropriately scale data analyses to the populations of interest.

Identification of the data that can be captured through automated methods will guide the implementation
of exposure monitoring, ensuring practical and achievable methods.

Accessible monitoring solutions that do not impede the user will be developed with a focus
on what is achievable based on an improved understanding of what contextual information is of value.

The goal is to establish what contextual information can be captured and to further develop the exposure
monitoring framework within these limitations.

5.4.1.3     Gap: Lack of Understanding of the Level of Fidelity Required for Blast Monitoring
The various existing strategies to estimate, predict, or measure blast loads on personnel do not yield the same
level of fidelity. Estimates based on static characterization of the blast environment may not account
for the effect of body posture and blast reflections. Wearable blast sensors can readily record local overpressure
histories at the location where they are worn, but the collected data may not accurately represent the loading
experienced at the head of the exposed personnel due to localized effects influenced by the posture of the body
relative to the propagation of the blast wave. Predictions of head loads obtained through numerical simulations
may also be limited to specific body postures.


5-8                                                                                                     STO-TR-HFM-338
                                                         RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT GUIDELINES


In order to successfully quantify the relationship between blast exposure (dose) and brain health effects
(response), an accurate characterization of the exposure is required.

Characterizing the relationship between estimates, numerical predictions, or wearable blast sensor measurements
– and the actual loads on the head of exposed personnel in an array of different exposure scenarios – will help
understand if additional transformation or processing is required to obtain a more accurate representation of blast
exposure.

More accurate estimations of personal exposure histories in longitudinal studies will help to identify correlations
between blast exposures and brain health effects, whereas imprecise characterization of exposures may hide
subtle trends and hinder the identification of proper exposure limits and injury thresholds. For monitoring
strategies, more accurate estimation of head loads following blast exposures will likely necessitate advanced
processing techniques that can digest and transform large quantities of data rapidly with a lower
computational cost.

5.4.1.4    Gap: Need for Improved Biological Dosimetry
New biological sensors or assays that monitor for the physiological effects of blast exposures need
to be developed. Biological dosimetry for monitoring blast exposure means assessment of associated effects
among exposed persons rather than direct measurement of blast overpressure during exposure events. At present,
there has not been a clearly defined and demonstrated reliable relationship between blast exposure dose
and biological response, although there has been research progress toward that end.

Having a clearly defined blast exposure dose and biological response relationship would provide a qualitatively
different method for monitoring blast exposure, complementing and adding flexibility to direct measurement
of exposure effects in field settings.

Developing biological dosimetry methods for blast exposure may be based on basic science with non-human
models but implementation will depend on some studies and validation with human subjects, acknowledging
that human subjects’ studies will be with lower-level exposures that occur in routine military training to follow
ethical standards for research. In these studies, both measurement of the exposure and degree of biological
response will need to be employed concurrently so that dose–response associations can be demonstrated
with evidence. It will be important to consider these associations as both a function of acute exposure
(e.g., single day of training) and of chronic exposure (e.g., across a military career of several years).

A biological dosimetry program could range from portable non-invasive assessments in acute exposure
environments (e.g., symptom reporting, behavioral performance evaluation, handheld biosample assay device)
to more remote assessments available in clinical treatment settings (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid sampling, advanced
neuroimaging). A corollary to a clinic-based biological dosimetry assessment program may be observed
in typical hearing conservation program, in which those enrolled are periodically evaluated for hearing ability
deficits instead of having their work environments quantitatively evaluated for noise level.

The type of approaches implemented for biological dosimetry in a blast monitoring program can be based
on effectiveness of measurement as well as logistical and practical considerations. Biological dosimetry
will be most informative and revealing when used in conjunction with direct measurement of exposure,
but effective biological dosimetry could also be employed independently when complementary measures
of exposure are not available or are too resource-intensive for implementation.

5.4.1.5    Gap: Need for Improved Methods to Capture Exposure History
There is a need/requirement to develop the methodology and infrastructure to document exposures to blast
overpressure events for military personnel to enable approaches to mitigate adverse cognitive and mental health
outcomes from blast exposures. This can be accomplished in several ways depending upon the institutional
infrastructure to support. A straightforward method to capture, record, and memorialize individual blast
exposures is with wearable blast sensors that permit recording of key blast exposure parameters that are then
logged into a system available to the operational units and medical communities. In the absence of wearable
blast sensors, the recording can be accomplished using static field sensors to characterize a group environment
exposure or having military personnel estimate the number and intensity of exposures. This latter method is
low-cost, readily deployable, and affords insight into cumulative exposures, but may pose limitations inherent
with self-reporting and memory recall.

Strategies to mitigate adverse neurological outcomes from repetitive exposure to blast overpressure events
associated with explosives and weapon systems necessarily depend upon accurate recording of exposure
parameters and then subsequent memorializing in operational and medical records systems.

The parameters of blast overpressure exposures, such as intensity, duration, and frequency, are presumed
to be of a fidelity to permit recording of essential elements of the blast exposure that can be linked to brain health
effects. The common data elements required to provide a high-fidelity blast record remain to be determined.
The approach of recording blast overpressure exposures in military personnel is not unlike
the systems/infrastructure used to record specific parameters for military divers that include dive duration, depth,
ascent rate from depth, water temperature, specifics around the diving equipment that is recorded and stored
at the operational unit level and in a health safety center charged with recording occupational exposures
for military personnel.

The goal of the military health system will be to institute a system to log blast exposures in a similar manner
that is available to operational units, medical providers, and health system analysts. A system is envisioned
to record blast exposures over the entirety of a service member’s career.

The establishment of a database record of exposure to blast overpressure events will enable continual analysis
of blast overpressure exposure guidelines to determine same thresholds and exposure conditions,
including repetitive exposures contributing to cumulative effects.

5.4.2     Research Focus Area: Load Mechanisms
Blast overpressure events exert various loading effects on the human body, which can be categorized
into primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary effects. Primary effects result directly from the initial
shockwave affecting the body, leading to barotrauma and brain injuries. Secondary effects are caused by flying
debris and fragments propelled by the blast, causing penetrating injuries, lacerations, amputations, and fractures.
Tertiary effects occur when individuals are thrown or impacted by the blast wave, resulting in blunt trauma,
concussions, and traumatic brain injuries. The load mechanisms, the phenomena, and the physical effects
of the blast, effectuating the adverse effect on the human body, need further research to achieve a comprehensive
and full understanding of the effects, their interaction, and potential superpositions. The two research gaps
are described in detail in the following section, which focus only on single exposure events (which might
comprise multiple, overlapping blast events) and not repetitive, cumulative exposures.

                                                          RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT GUIDELINES


5.4.2.1    Gap: Lack of Understanding of the Interaction of Blast Loading with Other Mechanisms
Non-blast-related environmental and operational factors may have interactions on the blast load which are not
well understood. Sources that may interact with the blast loads include local accelerative forces and momentums
(tertiary blast, e.g., firing long-range rifles, shoulder launched rockets), changes in atmospheric conditions due
to hot plumes, gasses and potentially toxic ingredients (quaternary blast), alterations of exposure due to body
posture (standing, crouching, horizontal), blast reflections, and multiple blast origins. Next to that, it is assumed
that, for example, the short-term dynamic compression of the upper body, the organs, and vessels located
in this area can generate additional cerebral effects that may have potential for injury.

The investigation of these interactions and interfering effects is of high importance, as they may both increase
or decrease the resulting load affecting the body. Further, understanding these effects will potentially enable
optimization of PPE and range design, and refine SOPs. All these interactions should be considered
in the holistic investigation of the injury risks for blast-related mTBI.

The general approach for the investigation of these interfering load mechanisms requires comprehensive
experimental investigations with generic, common set-ups, and scenarios and a well-tuned parameter matrix.
Various combinations of parameters should be studied to identify combinations that lead to worst-case exposures
(maximum of loads). Numerical investigations should also accompany the experiments to extend the results
beyond what is capable or feasible with experimental testing. The numerical models need to be efficient
and validated for the range of parameters studied. Experimental studies to analyze the phenomena
and the additional load mechanisms resulting from the load on the torso using animals, human cadavers, or other
surrogates should be part of the scientific approach.

The goal of these research efforts is to have a deeper understanding of the phenomena and the physics related
to the interactions of other mechanisms with the blast, next to the identification of reinforcing (worst-case)
and mitigating effects and scenarios.

The research filling this gap will help optimize training procedures, improve monitoring tools and procedures,
develop mitigation strategies with regards to range design (positions, structural layout, etc.), and weapon system
development and characterization of relevant parameters of the overall exposure resulting from a weapon
system. Further, cooperation with gaps related to injury mechanisms will help to identify consequences
of the adjusted loads on the subsequent, resulting injury mechanisms and the effects on the brain.

5.4.2.2    Gap: Lack of Understanding of the Sensitivity of the Brain to Blast Loading on the Body
Monitoring tools for operators like wearable blast sensors are typically attached to the operator’s equipment
at tactically, operationally, and ergonomically appropriate positions, e.g., chest, shoulder, and back of the helmet,
and tools like static sensors provide incident overpressure measurements at a limited number of locations.
For practical reasons, comprehensive measurements of the overpressure field across the entire head
of the operator are not collected, so the effective load on the head from a blast exposure must be inferred
from the available sensor measurement points. However, a validated transfer function enabling estimation
of the load on the brain based on sensor data (and knowledge of sensor positions, PPE worn, anthropometry,

accurate estimations of brain exposure load, as significant under or overestimations of exposure will compromise
exposure monitoring efforts and potentially lead to erroneous medical or training decisions regarding
the exposure status of the operator.

5.4.2.3    Gap: Lack of Predictive Modeling of Blast Exposures to Consider Future Combat Operations
Predictive modeling will assist in exploring and understanding the blast overpressure risk to the service member
with training and combat missions. Simulations can accommodate a wide range of blast threats and complex
environments and any number of potential locations within the environment that the soldier could be positioned.
This understanding enables planners and leadership to implement mitigating measures to reduce the blast
overpressure exposure of soldiers. Predictive models need to be verified and validated, ensuring realistic, applicable
results and a thorough understanding of the assumptions and limitations enabling sound interpretation of the results
as applicable to the mission. Predictive simulation models can consider many different loading conditions.

Although there are a wide range of blast damage and injury simulation tools, simulating human body
vulnerability and injury, both directly and indirectly within simple free-field and complex environments, these
programs are mostly limited to acute injuries. They do not appear to address injuries related to low-level
and/or repeated insults, especially from a wide range of threats such as muzzle and back blast from deployed
weaponry. This weakness is partly due to limited understanding of repeated low-level insults to the soldier
and related brain injury mechanisms, human tolerance, and acceptable injury risk limits. Although many gaps
still exist, predictive simulation models can easily be updated, verified, and validated as understanding grows.

Modifying existing or building the required predictive blast exposure models requires parameterized threats
and related overpressure source models, environmental models, and human models with appropriate material
models, load coupling, and reflection (impedance) models. Each of these requires verification and validation
separately and then as a complex combined unit using published standards where possible. Steps have already
been made to ensure the quantification of new weapons systems providing core model and verification data.
This should be expanded to cover legacy systems and, where possible, adversarial threats.

The quantified threat, material, and reflection model data are provided through tests using standardized
methodologies and, where possible, common instrumentation, positioning, and data processing techniques used
in accordance with relevant standards to ensure comparable data from a range of sources. The predictive models
should be verified against provided test data and then validated when initially applied to a training range
and/or missions (where possible). A key challenge of predictive models is to enable ease of use
and interpretation by non-specialist modelers while not compromising computational accuracy and applicability.

5.4.3     Research Focus Area: Injury Mechanisms
The fundamental question of how blast waves cause brain tissue mechanics that lead to injury is not fully
understood, highlighting the need for in-depth research to unravel the complexities of this phenomenon. Here,
the term “injury mechanism” refers to phenomena of mechanopathology, where the mechanics of brain tissue
caused by blast exposure will lead to dysfunction and pathology within that tissue. Injury mechanism
is the fundamental process which drives the correlation between the characteristics of blast exposure (the dose)
and adverse brain health effects (the response).

5.4.3.1    Gap: Biofidelic Surrogates for Blast Brain Injury Risk Analysis Do Not Exist
Currently, we lack the fundamental understanding of the relationship between the characteristics of blast
exposure and the internal biomechanical response of human brain tissue that will lead to adverse brain health
effects and injury risk. Observational studies involving human subjects are necessary for identifying correlations
between blast exposure and health outcomes, but these studies cannot establish causal mechanisms associated
with the outcome. As such, biofidelic surrogates are necessary to perform these studies. Biofidelic surrogates,
in both physical (e.g., dummies) and computational forms (e.g., finite element models), can identify better
correlates between the exposure and the health outcomes observed in these field studies, and can play a pivotal
role in elucidating the underlying mechanisms of blast-induced brain effects. While basic surrogates exist,
their biofidelity is insufficient to capture the intricacies of blast-induced brain responses. To enhance
these models, a comprehensive approach involving additional biomechanical research into the properties
and responses of brain tissue under blast loading is necessary. Complementary efforts, utilizing a synergy
of preclinical animal models and their corresponding computational models to simulate blast response,
are also needed to contribute to identifying the fundamental injury mechanisms that can be translated effectively
to the biofidelic surrogates. Fundamental injury mechanisms that lead to abnormalities such as hemorrhage,
axonal injuries, and contusion, can be defined using biomechanical models that provide injury risks from a given
exposure. With the development of biofidelic physical and computational models of human heads and brains,
we can study the tissue mechanics caused by blast exposure that eventually leads to neuropathology
and dysfunction. These surrogates need to be validated with field study data to verify their predictive capability.
Once the links between human surrogates, animal studies, and observational data are established and validated,
these biofidelic surrogates become powerful predictive tools. They could enable researchers to assess brain
effects under nearly limitless exposure conditions, providing valuable insights that are not feasible through
observational studies alone. This predictive capability may open new avenues for studying a wide range of blast
conditions and developing targeted interventions to mitigate the impact of blast-induced brain injuries.

5.4.3.2    Gap: Need for Preclinical/Translational Blast Exposure Models that Scale More Effectively
           to Humans
In-vivo preclinical animal models are a necessity for studying the injury mechanisms associated with blast
exposure, but most of the in-vivo animal models injury literature has focused on the use of rodent models
to study the neuropathology and behavioral changes following a blast exposure. Of these studies, there are few
(if any) that have been designed to evaluate injury outcome as a function of the exposure conditions (e.g., peak
pressure, positive impulse, duration). Furthermore, there is a debate about the relevance of small rodent models
and the blast conditions used in their study to the types of exposures expected with humans (either from training
or on duty). Translating the exposure conditions and injury outcomes of preclinical models to equivalent human
conditions is a biomechanical process known as scaling. For the relevance of the study, it is crucial that the
preclinical model is exposed to loading conditions that are appropriately scaled to blast conditions experienced
by operators. From a biophysical perspective, the closer the size of a species is to a human, the more effective
the scaling process is in relating preclinical models to humans. Studies using medium- (e.g., ferret, rabbit) or
large-sized animals (e.g., pig) have evaluated injury outcomes as a function of the blast parameters, but more
research is needed in these models to translate into the robust scaling processes. The use of computational
surrogates of animals and humans is also critical in identifying the scaling process to equate preclinical and
operational loading conditions because they can account for the vast differences in anatomical structure, shape,
and size of the heads, skulls, and brains of different species. Identifying better preclinical models of blast
exposure and the related health effects, along with the knowledge of appropriate scaling processes, will ensure
that preclinical studies, utilizing animals and appropriate loading conditions, are more likely to translate to
human conditions. Low-cost rodent models may still be useful in a blast injury research program if the
experiments are designed properly with relevant exposure conditions, and that they are used as a stepping-stone
towards refining the study scope with larger animal testing. Coupling appropriately designed preclinical models
with their corresponding computational models will contribute to identifying the fundamental injury mechanisms
of blast brain injury and can elevate the capability of human surrogates for predicting brain health effects from
blast exposure.

5.4.3.3    Gap: Lack of Understanding of the Injury Mechanisms of Acute Blast Exposure
There is a need to elucidate the physiological changes that occur with acute exposure to blast overpressure
that contribute to adverse brain health effects with repetitive exposure. While there is a wealth of information
on the acute effects of blast overpressure that result in overt trauma, much less is known about the acute
physiological effects of lower-intensity blast overpressure exposures that contribute to long-term adverse
cognitive and mental health outcomes. An understanding of the acute physiological manifestations resulting
from blast exposure will contribute to a better understanding of these events and how they contribute
to cumulative effects with repeated exposure over a military career.

A schema of blast exposure separates the characteristics of blast overpressure from primary to quinary injuries
resulting from acute exposure to varying blast overpressure intensities. The principal concern around cumulative
effects is understanding the acute manifestations of the blast wave with the focus then on the neurological effects
of repetitive exposures that do not produce obvious acute effects but could result in subclinical injuries that may
accumulate with subsequent exposures. The challenge is to determine the characteristics of the blast exposure,
peak overpressure intensity and/or duration, and exposure frequency that contribute to these acute manifestations
that can result in cumulative effects. Molecular and neuroimaging techniques for investigating military brain
health/injury resulting from blast exposure have shown promising results in yielding potential biomarkers
to inform exposures that lead to cumulative effects.

5.4.3.4    Gap: Lack of Understanding of the Injury Mechanisms of Repetitive Blast Exposure
There is a need to elucidate the physiological changes that occur with repetitive exposure to blast overpressure
that leads to cumulative adverse brain health effects.

There is currently a lack of understanding regarding the injury mechanisms of repetitive exposure
to low-intensity blast overpressure in military members. This gap in understanding hampers effective diagnosis,
mitigation, and treatment strategies, and is hindered by the complexity of blast wave injury mechanisms
and inadequate longitudinal studies. These mechanisms are particularly important in the context of subclinical
injury following blast exposure. In this context, brain tissue damage with associated pathophysiological cascades
may occur following blast exposure, which do not necessarily rise to the level of a clinical manifestation.
Incompletely resolved subclinical injury may accumulate with subsequent exposures, resulting in long-term
clinical manifestations. It is critical to understand the precise mechanisms by which injury occurs to better
understand mitigation and treatment strategies for this condition.

Military members/Veterans with a history of repetitive head trauma – including, symptomatic concussions, mild
traumatic brain injuries, or repeated subclinical injury attributable to low-intensity explosive blast waves –
exhibit measurable performance decrements and adverse health outcomes. It is imperative to better understand,
detect, monitor, and mitigate brain dysfunction to optimize health and performance, thereby preserving readiness
and after-service life.

The injury mechanisms behind the brain dysfunction observed in those individuals exposed to repetitive blast
overpressure and resulting chronic neurological disorders are still not well understood, especially in terms
of persistent mental health consequences and risk of long-term neurodegenerative disorders. Promoting
optimal brain health requires scientific evaluation of complex aspects of neurological function.
The most promising avenues of investigation are in the neuroimaging and biomolecular domains. Molecular
and neuroimaging techniques present distinct but complementary approaches for investigating military brain
health/injury and have yielded potential biomarkers. Integration of these multimodal technologies employing
data-driven Artificial Intelligence-enabled Machine Learning-based data analytics techniques could advance
models of disease pathobiology, prognosis, ultimately improving precision care for subclinical and/or clinical
neurotrauma. Future studies should strive to integrate multiple imaging modalities to further address
outstanding neuroscientific problems.

Further investigation of the mechanisms of repetitive blast-related brain disorders/diseases may elucidate
approaches for mitigation, treatment, and improved brain function. Brain imaging-based cognitive neuroscience
and molecular tracers may unravel the underlying brain mechanism of cognitive dysfunction and/or progressive
neurodegeneration and provide an avenue to develop a biological framework for precision biomarkers.

By elucidating the gaps in understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms of repetitive low-intensity blast
exposure, advancements in guidelines can be made to enhance mitigation measures and optimize treatment

5.4.3.5    Gap: Lack of Understanding of How Environmental Factors Influence the Injury Response
           to Blast Exposure
Unlike the civilian population, the military is required by the mission to act in extreme environments.
These extreme conditions may relate to external factors (temperature, hygrometry) or to internal factors (lack
of sleep, high cognitive load, external stressors management, pain, intensive physical effort). In other conditions,
such as PTSD and TBI, these factors are known to trigger negative coping/adaptation strategies which favor
negative outcomes in the short- and long-term. Having control over the combined load of these factors,
as well as understanding individual contributions, allows for short- and long-term protection of the warfighter
brain, protecting the mission and operational effectiveness. It is therefore recommended to study the role
of these factors in the brain health response to blast exposure and prioritize by level of importance. Additionally,
it is recommended to quantify warfighter exposure to these factors to better understand risk to brain health
in the setting of blast exposure. It is further recommended to study acute and long-term outcomes associated
with these factors in the setting of blast exposure. Once the influence of environmental factors is known, counter
measures may be developed and further studied.

5.4.4     Research Focus Area: Injury Risk Function and Thresholds
The mitigation of adverse brain health effects hinges on a detailed understanding of the relationship between
blast exposure dosage and human physiological response. Injury Risk Functions (IRFs, also called an injury
criteria) are statistical relationships that link a quantity of exposure dose to the risk of occurrence of an injury
or negative health outcome. It is a type of dose–response relationship with a focus on a specific metric (dose)
and the risk of a specific outcome (response). An injury threshold may be derived from an IRF by identifying
the dose value associated with a level of acceptable risk.

5.4.4.1    Gap: Incomplete Understanding of Blast Exposure Brain Injury Criteria to Establish Risk
There is limited evidence regarding the physical metric that best captures the aspects of blast exposure
that are relevant to the risk of adverse brain health effects from repeated low-level exposures. Blast parameters
such as peak overpressure or positive-phase impulse have been proposed as metrics for single exposure injury,
which reflects the current state of science, but it is unclear if they are the best correlates for repetitive exposures.
In addition, it is unclear if the best metrics for predicting acute effects from repetitive blast exposure during a short
training period (up to a few days) are also the best for describing the risk of chronic effects from the accumulation
of blast exposure over a career. Identifying the most relevant injury metric is crucial to develop adequate blast
exposure monitoring strategies and select adequate monitoring technologies (i.e., the metric would need
to be derived from quantities that are measurable or predictable). Addressing this gap is also on the critical path to
the development of robust injury risk functions that will enable injury risk predictions and effective mitigation.

The general approach to solving this gap relies on robust study design and the establishment of common
measurement and analysis techniques across multiple studies. Studies will need to include a characterization
of blast exposure at the individual level, which will enable the calculation of multiple metrics from measured
or predicted pressure histories. In parallel, relevant health data will need to be collected from those individuals
and their effects documented. Statistical modeling of the most appropriate metrics should be made from a series

5.4.4.2    Gap: Incomplete Understanding of Blast Exposure Thresholds for the Acute Brain Health
           Effects of Blast Exposure
Evidence-based blast exposure thresholds for the occurrence of acute brain health effects are limited
in validation. The characteristics and minimum quantity of blast exposures that may lead to acute effects
remain tentative. Also, different exposure thresholds may be linked to different health effects. A more
robust identification of blast exposure injury thresholds will enable updates to current safety limits to
mitigate acute brain health effects of blast exposure. Solving this gap will require a statistical analysis to
identify or estimate which metric of a cumulative exposure is associated with an onset of risk of acute
effects. This process may focus on the identification of an acute threshold alone, or on the definition of
a complete injury risk function, from which thresholds may be inferred or extracted.

Acute injury thresholds are critical to the development of exposure guidelines across all weapon systems
or explosive scenarios, as it represents the limit that should not be exceeded to keep military personnel free
of acute health effects and at peak operational readiness. It will enable decision-makers, responsible
for members’ safety and health, to understand the level of risk involved with certain frequencies of training
on various weapon systems and with various explosive charges.

5.4.4.3    Gap: Incomplete Understanding of Blast Exposure Thresholds for the Chronic Brain Health

This information is critical to enable the mitigation of chronic effects through better management of individual

the important features of individual blast exposure history. A cumulative metric for chronic effects may not have
the same resolution as those used in the context of acute effects. However, the approach toward defining
a cumulative threshold will use similar statistical analysis to defining acute thresholds by correlating values

5.4.6     Research Focus Area: Detection of the Brain Health Correlates of Blast Effects

5.4.6.3    Gap: Incomplete Understanding of the Effects of Blast Exposure on Operational

that occur with repeated exposures, may be relevant to service member performance of warfighter tasks,
and deficits may have consequences in safety or mission execution, which would be important for unit leader

A subclinical blast exposure-induced performance deficit that has consequences in safety or mission execution
has a direct relevance that may be much more salient for unit leaders than a physiological effect or performance
effect on a task designed for research purposes.

Developing an assessment directly related to operational performance is a cornerstone in the practice
of Occupational Medicine and principles from that medical discipline can be used to identify methods
to evaluate blast exposure effects on real-world behavioral performance. This approach may involve a different
assessment for each occupation instead of a single assessment that generalizes across all. As an example, blast
exposure-induced slowing of decision-making by a service member may be more important for some
occupational specialties than others, and understanding such subclinical occupational effects can be helpful
in relative risk management.

Unit leader decision-making can be supported when transient behavioral effects from acute exposure to blast
are communicated in terminology of actual tasks required for training or mission execution effectiveness.

The direct relevance of blast exposure effects on activities already salient and important for unit leaders can
promote application of blast monitoring guidelines. Consideration of blast exposure effects on operational
performance is much more relevant to acute exposure and transient effects than to chronic exposure and effects
associated with diagnosable injury.

This gap addresses the development and implementation of a longitudinal approach that can follow
blast-exposed military personnel and controls during and after their military service.

Currently, there exists inadequate longitudinal data describing the neurological effects of blast exposure
in military personnel over their career. Much of the knowledge on the brain health effects of repeated low-level
blast exposure to date has been learned from cross-sectional studies and retrospective medical chart reviews.
While there is much that can be learned from those kinds of studies, and they do allow for the generation
of actionable near-term knowledge, there is value in the availability of longitudinal data. A need exists
for establishing support systems to generate longitudinal data in blast-exposed military personnel to help account
for the influence of individual difference on the brain health effects of exposures over a career. Such approaches
would need to be capable of spanning MoD/DoD and veteran systems. Longitudinal data collection could occur
as a part of a prospective longitudinal study with a long-term commitment to the collection of key brain health
and exposure data over a career. Such an approach would likely be supported by research teams and could
involve the collection of research as well as clinical measures over a career. Conversely, an alternate
or complementary approach could involve collection of data into a clinical registry that would allow
for the structured collection and storage of information acquired as a part of the standard of care for a service
member. The database of information collected through this registry could be queried to explore the brain health
effects of blast exposure. Critical to any approach would be the incorporation of blast exposure measures
to allow for an understanding of the exposure history of everyone over their career. The availability of such data
could help inform a broad range of solutions for the optimization of brain health in military personnel exposed
to blast over a career.

5.4.7     Research Focus Area: Mitigation and Intervention Strategies
The purpose of this Research Focus Area is to effectively and efficiently direct research to provide the required
inputs for personal protective equipment, specifications for test environment design, and exposure reduction
both before and during service. Introduction of test standards, supported by research, that quantify blast effects
will ensure the development and application of suitable test and evaluation methods that will drive the design
of better equipment, training programs, and facilities as well as drive the design of weapons systems to reduce
the effects of blast loading on the brain.

5.6.7.1    Gap: Lack of Blast Specific Design Criteria for Personal Protective Equipment to Mitigate
           the Effects of Blast Exposure
The design, development, and evaluation of personal protective equipment has primarily focused on non-blast
threats, specifically to protect from ballistic trauma, and secondary blast injury (caused by fragments).
This results in equipment that is not optimized and may not be effective in addressing blast loading mechanisms
on the service member.

To drive the development of better equipment that results in greater protection of the warfighter from blast
exposures in training and operational scenarios, design, and test standards for personal protective equipment
that includes blast-related mitigation and protection assessment testing need to be developed. This research
will provide personal protective equipment modified to reduce the effects of blast while retaining current ballistic
protection. Generating loading data during research, development, and testing will increase understanding of how
this equipment can be used to reduce or eliminate transmission of blast loading on the brain.

This research should provide an understanding of what criteria and methods are important and applicable
to reduce blast loading on the brain and should thus be used to design and assess personal protective equipment.
This can also influence doctrinal use of existing and new equipment to provide improved blast load reduction.

Data and understanding resulting from improved personal protective equipment development, standardized
assessments combined with training, field applications, and user feedback will identify areas for operational
guideline improvement and potential future standardization.

5.4.7.2    Gap: Lack of Adequate Specifications for the Assessment and Design of the Training
           Environment to Reduce Brain Health Effects
Current concepts for the construction and operation of training facilities (ranges, breacher houses, etc.)
are mainly limited to the safety design and durability of the infrastructure as well as the functional realization
of general military-relevant safety regulations. However, there are no existing guidelines for the design
of the training environment that mitigate potential overpressure exposure. Next to that, the mitigating influence
of different infrastructure (e.g., cover, enclosed environment geometry), other equipment on ranges
and operational procedures (e.g., requirement to open selected windows/doors/relieve openings or to remove
selected reflecting objects in the facility) to reduce exposures to all individuals present in the vicinity is not yet
sufficiently quantified and available to improve range design.

The use of many tactical applications and weapon systems is associated with explosive energy releases.
In unfavorable cases, the resulting effects can be amplified by the immediate surroundings (infrastructure)
through reflection in general or adverse superposition of the peaks in a complex reflection geometry.

One approach could be to develop easy-to-use tools for carrying out analyses and assessments of training
environments regarding the propagation of blast waves and the visualization of the overpressure color map
throughout the environment. Functional steps may include 3D scanning of the environment, characterization
and definition of the weapon system, simulation and visualization of the effects, and when necessary, supported
by on-site measurements of the blast loads.

Users could thus reduce the amplified effects by temporarily modifying the exercise plans or making permanent
infrastructural changes. The installation of monitoring tools/sensors on the training infrastructure, at predefined
and suitable positions, to continuously monitor the blast load could support mitigation and monitoring approaches.

Prerequisites would be, for example, the availability of data (pressure development/propagation)
of the respective weapon systems. This requires uniform test standards, as outlined in Section 5.4.7.3.

5.4.7.3    Gap: Inadequate Testing Standards to Quantify Weapon System Blast Load on the Brain
Weapon systems are a major contributor to blast overpressure exposure of military personnel. Qualification
of these systems are not focused on minimizing the blast loading on users and nearby personnel, thus requiring
post blast quantification to enable optimized training and operational use to minimize the blast overpressure
exposure to the operators.

The implementation of standards that include testing of weapons systems will result in limiting the overpressure
experienced by the operator and ensure that the brain health of the operator is considered during the development
and employment of weapon systems. This will in turn allow for more frequent training, as the operator will not
be as restricted to current training limitations due to excessive blast exposure.

Standardized procedures for capturing exposure data for weapon systems should be defined, with overpressure
thresholds defined relative to preserving brain health to enable comparisons between different weapon systems
and munitions. Development of generally applicable test procedures for the analysis of different weapon systems or

RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT GUIDELINES


tactical applications provided to weapons manufacturers will ensure that a reduction in overpressure to the operator
is a prime consideration in the development of new weapons, and in the refinement of existing weapons.

Providing adequate test standards for weapon systems will ultimately protect the warfighter, enable increased
training frequency, and lead to the development of improved safety for the operators of these weapons.
In addition, the use of weapons that result in high overpressure exposure that potentially the most injurious can
be identified and phased out of service. Standards that are used in procurement will ultimately force weapon
manufacturers to adhere to safety protocols and consider the operator and not just the on-target effects.

Developing a testing standard for each class of weapon will enable comparisons between weapon systems where
operator brain health is a prominent evaluation criterion for the procurement of weapons.

5.4.7.4    Gap: Lack of Objective Data to Inform Techniques that Best Reduce Blast Exposure
In most of the military training taking place today, the absence of objective data informing opportunities
for technique adjustments that reduce blast exposure hinders instructors’ and trainees’ ability to test and confirm
hypotheses involving the best place to stand, best equipment to wear, best weapon firing posture, etc.
The current state-of-the-art treatment for reducing brain health effects is mitigation, so any adjustments
in training that can reduce exposures are critical.

More objective data from training, and subsequent information sharing, is needed. Troops must be given tools,
like blast sensors, to measure their exposure, make changes, then remeasure to see the effect. If blast exposure
is lower with the new method, and the efficacy of training is not reduced, then this learning must be
communicated as widely as possible.

Troops must have the tools and education to adjust their tactics, techniques, and procedures that reduce blast
exposure, promoting effectiveness in the field and long-term brain health for the force. This gap is core to any
exposure guideline. As it is addressed, troops will increase their ability to operate safely and remain under blast
exposure guidelines while maintaining, or improving, the efficacy of their training.

5.4.7.5    Gap: Inadequate Pre-Exposure Health-Related Mitigation Approaches
The effects of pre-exposure factors that can reduce adverse brain health effects of blast exposure are not
well understood and require further study to mitigate health effects of repetitive blast exposure.

Scant evidence exists of pre-exposure prophylactic interventions against acute or chronic adverse health
outcomes from blast exposure. Use of nutraceuticals or behavioral interventions, such as avoiding psychoactive
substances and sleep deprivation, can possibly reduce the severity of brain health effects. Explorative research,
such as basic research, preclinical studies, cross-sectional studies, or cohort studies, are needed to discover
methods that are effective. Specifically, basic research and preclinical studies can help the discovery
of mitigation strategies that target biological protective mechanisms rather than mitigating symptoms.
High-quality randomized controlled trials are necessary to determine which approaches have a significant effect
in (military) exposure scenarios. Additionally, research on mitigation strategies should differentiate between
acute or chronic blast exposure, so strategies are developed for both types of exposure. If proven effective,
these approaches can directly mitigate brain health consequences from blast exposure and mitigate use
of ineffective substances or methods that cost time and resources while providing a false sense of security.
Moreover, developments in this research area contribute to advancement of the capability “Mitigations and
Medical Interventions” by providing improved options for protective measures.

5.4.7.6    Gap: Inadequate Health-Related Mitigation Approaches Post-Exposure
There is a need to develop therapeutic approaches to treat and/or mitigate adverse brain health effects following
blast exposure. Rehabilitative strategies for reducing and adjusting to long-term symptoms of blast exposure
need to be investigated, including determination of the optimum recovery time before return to duty
and consideration of co-occurring conditions.

Preclinical studies show several possible avenues for mitigating adverse brain health consequences from blast
exposure through post-exposure interventions, such as behavioral interventions, pharmaceuticals, or education
and supportive interventions. There is a lack of high-quality studies of sufficient sample size investigating
the effectiveness of these options. Explorative research is necessary to develop post-exposure interventions
that enhance recovery from blast exposure or prevent escalation from acute to chronic injury states and cumulative
injury. Time since exposure should be considered when assessing the effectiveness of interventions, to not only
find methods that work directly after the exposure, but also during following time periods and chronic injury states.
Similarly, as in Section 5.4.7.5, basic and preclinical research can contribute to finding interventions that target
injury and recovery mechanisms rather than symptoms. Large-scale prospective clinical trials are needed
to consolidate the results from exploratory research into evidence-based post-exposure prophylaxis methods,
which can be used in Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) that are applicable to specific (military) exposure
scenarios. Furthermore, these interventions and SOPs may be integrated into the “blast exposure monitoring”
and “health analysis” sub-capabilities outlined in this report to enable that when a certain exposure threshold
is reached fast and effective healthcare measures will be provided.

5.4.8     Research Focus Area: Data Elements, Processing, and Analysis

5.4.8.1    Gap: Inadequate Methods for Transmission of Common Data Elements to Exposure and
           Medical Records Within Authorized Databases
To achieve a better understanding of blast exposure and its resulting health consequences, it is necessary
to record and transfer exposure data to medical records and secured databases using standardized formats
and/or common data elements. However, there is a current gap in the methods to transfer exposure data
and adequate health data from in-field/training situations to relevant medical records and databases. Further,
a lack of an established standardization of this data will inhibit the use of AI-driven data analysis and research
across this high-dimensional data.

Development of approaches for transmission of exposure and health effects data to medical records
and authorized databases is needed. The goal of this research is to enable better use of recorded data for research
and development of future mitigation strategies but also for direct, in-training/deployed setting, feedback
for optimal recovery/rehabilitation. Medical records should be standardized via common data elements between
troops and member countries to enable collection and analysis of large datasets. Such data should be made
available and useful for different end users: from documentation of service member exposures to use
in short- and long-term clinical/public health-related matters as well as with industry partners. The collection
of this kind of data will need to be automated as much as possible to minimize workload for soldiers and health
care professionals in the collection of data. A fast, high through-put, high resolution data collection and storage
of blast waveform and health data and transfer of such data with common datasets/technical platforms between
different troops and different member countries MoD/DoD’s and their affiliated health care system
will contribute to virtually all other Research Focus Areas listed in this report.

5.4.8.2    Gap: Lack of Adequate Data Science Tools for Understanding and Managing Adverse Brain
           Health Effects of Blast Exposure
Data science plays a critical role in addressing military occupational brain health risks associated with repetitive
blast exposure. This interdisciplinary field contributes across multiple domains, including the acquisition,
processing, and organization of information; the creation of advanced decision-support tools;
and the identification of latent patterns in research data to facilitate new knowledge discovery. However,
there is a pressing need for tailored data science tools to address the unique challenges posed by repeated blast
exposures, which can have profound and long-lasting effects on service members’ brain health.

Data Acquisition and Organization: The refinement of large language models and related technologies
is essential for managing and organizing unstructured data generated in operational environments.
This unstructured data can include reports, sensor readings, and environmental measurements. Automated
processes should be developed to curate and store this information at both the individual level – tracking specific
service members – and the group level, enabling analysis of broader patterns across units or the entire force.
Furthermore, integrating advanced data techniques to combine exposure data with other known risk factors,
such as pre-existing health conditions or environmental variables, could significantly enhance the precision
of screening methods. These innovations would allow for more accurate, individualized risk assessments
and broader insights into systemic vulnerabilities.

Data Analysis and Integration: To maximize the utility of acquired data, advanced analytical techniques must
be developed and refined. Dimensionality reduction methods are particularly important to address the false
discovery issues that arise when working with expansive and diverse datasets. Such techniques can help filter
noise and highlight meaningful patterns, enabling more robust and reliable analysis. Additionally,
there is an urgent need for improvements in multimodal data integration, creating a unified analytical space
that can incorporate data from various sources – such as clinical records, operational reports, and sensor data –
into a cohesive framework. This capability would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how
repetitive blast exposures interact with other factors to impact brain health.

Unsupervised machine learning approaches are another key area for development. These techniques can uncover
hidden patterns within datasets, providing insights into relationships and trends that may not be immediately
apparent. Applying these methods to blast exposure data could reveal previously unrecognized risk factors
or pathways of injury, offering new directions for intervention and prevention.

Closing the Gaps: To address these challenges, the military and its research partners should prioritize
the development and application of advanced data science tools. Leveraging existing datasets from operational,
clinical, and research sources is a vital first step. These datasets, enriched with real-world insights
from blast-exposed populations, can serve as a foundation for creating more effective analytical tools.
Simultaneously, direct investment in cutting-edge technologies – such as machine learning, natural language
processing, and high-dimensional data analysis – will enable the creation of solutions tailored to the military’s
unique needs. By aligning these efforts with broader advancements in the data sciences, the military can develop
a comprehensive approach to mitigating the brain health risks associated with repetitive blast exposure.

Through these measures, data science can serve as a transformative force, driving innovation in prevention,
diagnosis, and treatment strategies for military personnel affected by blast exposure.

                                       Chapter 6 – SUMMARY

6.1 INTRODUCTION
While injury from blast exposures has been an important topic of research historically, research has largely
focused on combat or incident related injuries. This has resulted in the development of injury models
for e.g., assessment of lethality due to lung injuries and eardrum ruptures. More recent missions of NATO
members and allied countries in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Mali showed an increase in blast exposures during
combat and increased concerns for brain related injuries. A US and Australian deployment of wearable blast
sensors revealed significant exposures from firing heavy weapons in theatre, creating an awareness
of the repetitive overpressure threat from weapon systems in the training environment [1]. Increased rates
of traumatic brain injury triggered a response to expand screening and clinical care programs as well as research
investments. This resulted in more attention to the total burden of blast exposures on military personnel. Some
sentinel research conducted in the US began to explore the hypothesis that blast exposure could result in brain
health changes similar to those seen with diagnosed mild TBIs (concussions). US Navy Special Warfare cohorts
and specifically with the Carl Gustaf recoilless rifle, which showed transient neurocognitive changes, balance
disturbances, and complaints of headaches after training exercises. A 2018 review of US Marines who fired
an unusually high number of artillery rounds while deployed for six months found that these service members
suffered a higher rate of traumatic brain injuries than the rest of the artillery community [2]. Interest and research
expanded from combat blast-related TBIs to repetitive low level blast exposures occurring in the training
environments. Concerns for both acute and chronic adverse brain health effects led to more research
investigations to explore the relationship between blast exposures and acute and chronic brain health effects.

There are large differences in attention to the long-term brain health effects across NATO members and allied
countries. While several NATO members and allied countries were performing multiple years of research
in multiple fields, other countries were just getting started with this topic. Most of the research is still ongoing
and the first practical solutions have started to form. At the beginning of RTG HFM-338 a shared NATO
approach on how to address this current topic with a set of guidelines was missing.

As stated in the introduction, the strategy to cope with the risks of blast exposure and adverse brain health
outcomes within a military setting is based on a three-stage development of guidelines (expert-based,
knowledge-based, and experience-based) in the upcoming 15 – 25 years.


6.2 OUTCOMES OF HFM-338
RTG HFM-338 has defined a set of expert-based guidelines, reflecting the state of science, defined
by the experts in the field who have biophysics, biomedical, and/or operational backgrounds. The countries
contributing to the HFM-338 were Canada, France, Germany, Norway, South Africa, Sweden, The Netherlands,
United Kingdom, and The United States.

The intent of the guidelines contained in this report are to be user-friendly, practical, and dynamic. It is expected
that these guidelines are a starting point by which NATO members and allied nations may begin to address
critical issues related to repetitive blast exposures, brain health effects, and impacts to operational readiness
and performance. These guidelines attempt to provide ambitious but necessary goals to position NATO members
and allied nations for the future fight.

SUMMARY


The Interim Exposure guidelines provided the state of science on blast exposure threshold values. These values
may be used to mitigate the risk of adverse brain health effects due to blast exposure. Four different methods
were identified, and recommendations were proposed on how these can be used in future assessments.

The Operational guidelines provide a wide range of practical recommendations, which will support prevention
activities aimed at reducing adverse brain health effects. The four capabilities of (1) Blast Exposure Monitoring,
(2) Capture of Health and Performance Information and Data, (3) Analysis of Blast Exposure, Health,
and Performance Effects, and (4) Mitigation and Health Management Strategies set the structure
of these guidelines. Each capability includes sub-capabilities further detailing the operational guidelines.
Each sub-capability is presented in an ordinal fashion, starting with Level 1 through Level 6. Level 1 proposes
basic activities and progresses through Level 6, which would represent a mature and comprehensive solution
to the specific area discussed. The Operational guidelines were provided in a table format, providing a short
overview of the guidelines as well as in a more detailed narrative, providing the details required to start
implementing these guidelines. NATO members and allied countries are now armed with the state-of-the-science
and available data to address and mitigate military occupational brain health risks from repetitive blast exposures.
This report sets conditions to mitigate the effect of blast exposures today while positioning NATO members and
allied countries to leverage additional research and mitigation capability development for tomorrow.

The Research and Development guidelines present eight targeted Research Focus Areas with accompanying
gaps and goals. The guidelines provide insight into what research is necessary to make improvements
in the Exposure and Operational guidelines. These guidelines will allow nations to direct their research
to the most urgent topics.


6.3 RECOMMENDATIONS
The operational guidelines were defined in four capabilities, thirteen different sub-capabilities
each at six different levels. While a great level of detail is included in these guidelines, they are not specified
up to NATO member or allied country requirements. Since each country will have differences
in the organisation of military leadership, medical command, and the training environment, the Operational
guidelines will need to be implemented and specified to each country’s requirements accordingly. Only when
this step has been taken, the Operational guidelines can be fully implemented and aid in limiting the adverse
brain health outcomes from blast exposures.

The multiple nation collaboration in HFM-338 to formulate evidence-based guidelines provides insight
into the sheer magnitude of effort of research needed to protect and mitigate from adverse brain health effects
due to blast exposures. It shows a multiple nation research effort, and further alignment of research programs
are required to ensure research results will be available as soon as possible to limit the amount of military
personnel who develop adverse brain health effects. As such, it is vital to enable future collaborative research
across countries, starting with topics like common data elements and data sharing.

The Research and Development guidelines have listed 29 research gaps and goals, which require further
research. These may be used to select and direct future research in this field. While no prioritisation
has been made for these research gaps, it is recommended that each country starts selecting the most opportune
research goals and research on these gaps can be started.

  14. Abstract

    Blast overpressure exposure poses a significant occupational risk to military personnel and can
    cause negative effects to Force Health and Readiness. Most blast exposure occurs during training,
    where repeated low‑level blast differs from single high‑intensity blasts but may cumulatively
    harm brain health. Monitoring and documenting blast exposure is therefore essential to
    understanding long‑term effects and protecting warfighters throughout and beyond their careers.
    The RTG HFM‑338 developed a three-stage development of guidelines strategy to mitigate
    military occupational brain health risks from repetitive blast exposure. These three stages are
    expert-based (current), knowledge-based, and experience-based. The current report contains the
    expert‑based guidelines. The group produced three products: 1) The Interim Exposure Guidelines
    define expert‑based exposure thresholds, identifying limits for peak overpressure, cumulative
    impulse, and generalized blast exposure values. 2) The Operational Guidelines provide a
    structured framework for implementing a national blast exposure mitigation program, covering
    monitoring, health and performance data collection, analysis, and mitigation strategies, with
    multiple implementation levels. 3) The Research and Development Guidelines identify critical
    knowledge gaps across eight focus areas to advance understanding and long‑term protection of
    brain health. These expert based guidelines can be adopted and implemented by NATO and allied
    partners to mitigate military occupational brain health risks.
